The four relevant textual traditions are Aramaic, Greek, Ethiopic (Geʿez), and Latin. They do not all preserve the same amount of the description.

## Further detail and dating qualifications

1. Aramaic — 4Q204

The date is based on the handwriting, not the date of composition. A recent Brill handbook gives 33–1 BCE, while other palaeographic assessments have allowed a somewhat wider range. The relevant fragment is numbered 5 i in the older numbering system. Drawnel's 2020 edition should be consulted for the updated fragment arrangement and reading.

- J. T. Milik, The Books of Enoch: Aramaic Fragments of Qumran Cave 4 (1976).
- Henryk Drawnel, Qumran Cave 4: The Aramaic Books of Enoch (2020).
- Stuckenbruck, 1 Enoch 91–108 (2007), pp. 615–627.

The Brill handbook's manuscript dating table gives the 4Q204 date, and Stuckenbruck identifies the passage preserved in fragment 5 i.&#x20;

2. Greek — Chester Beatty–Michigan papyrus

This is one codex whose surviving leaves are divided between two collections, not two independent Greek witnesses.

- Dublin: Chester Beatty Library, BP XII.
- Ann Arbor: University of Michigan, P.Mich. inv. 5552–5553.
- The papyrus is catalogued as a fourth-century manuscript; the Leuven Database of Ancient Books gives 301–400 CE, with a narrower 300–350 dating also reported.
- It contains Enoch 97:6–104:13 and 106:1–107:3.

The papyrus is particularly important because it preserves the Greek Noah description independently of the much later Ethiopic manuscript copies.&#x20;

Editions:

- Matthew Black, ed., Apocalypsis Henochi Graece (1970).

3. Ethiopic — Geʿez manuscripts

Here we must distinguish the date of the translation from the dates of the manuscripts we possess.

The Geʿez translation is generally placed in the early period of Ethiopic Christian literature, approximately 4th–7th centuries CE. The oldest securely identified surviving manuscripts are much later, principally fifteenth-century copies.

Some important manuscripts are:

| EMML 2080                          | 15th century; possibly earlier | Knibb and later manuscript research        |
| Abbadianus 55                      | 15th or 16th century           | Knibb, manuscript catalogue                |

## A final chronological distinction

The Birth of Noah composition itself is older than its surviving Qumran copy. Stuckenbruck places its Enochic form between approximately the middle of the second century BCE and the last third of the first century BCE. Peters likewise dates it to before the middle of the first century BCE.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://dokumen.pub\&sz=32)

For our colour investigation, the evidence hierarchy is therefore:

1. 4Q204: earliest physical witness, but the crucial white and rose wording is lost and editorially reconstructed.
2. Greek papyrus: earliest substantially complete physical witness to the white/red/rose description.
3. Geʿez: complete transmission, but surviving copies are medieval.
4. Latin: early medieval witness to the story, but it omits the actual colour comparison.

The Greek papyrus is consequently our earliest surviving direct evidence for the combination of whiteness and redness. The Aramaic is earlier evidence for the underlying text and for the surviving red adjective, but it cannot independently confirm the complete colour phrase.

That is the distinction I would preserve in any future table or argument.
