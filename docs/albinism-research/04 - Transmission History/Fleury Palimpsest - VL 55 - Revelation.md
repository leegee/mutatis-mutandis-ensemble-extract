# Fleury **Palimpsest** – VL 55 – Paris, BnF, Latin 6400 G

## Text

**Principal edition:** E. S. Buchanan, _The Four Gospels from the Codex Corbeiensis ... together with fragments of the Catholic Epistles, of the Acts and of the Apocalypse from the Fleury Palimpsest_ (Oxford, 1907), especially the Fleury section, beginning p. 97.

### Provisional transcription from Buchanan's searchable OCR

```
... et in medio candelabrorum similem filio hominis
uestitum podere et erat praecinctus super mamillas
zonam auream

caput autem eius et capilli erat [= erant?]
uelut la]na aut nix et oculi eius ut flamma ignis
et pedes eius similes aurocalco sicut de fornace igneo
et uos [= uox?] eius ut sonus aquarum multarum
...
```

**Status: provisional, not a diplomatic transcription.**

The OCR is unreliable in precisely the places that matter:

- `erat` may be a misrecognition of `erant`. The plural is supported by the parallel Latin tradition, but Buchanan's printed page still needs checking.
    
- `la]na` indicates damage or editorial supplementation in the OCR representation. The printed edition's brackets and conventions must be inspected.
    
- `uos` is almost certainly an OCR error for `uox` (voice).
    
- `igneo` needs checking against the printed page. It may represent an unusual manuscript reading, an editorial form, or an OCR error.
    

The feet-and-furnace wording is independently reported from Buchanan's p. 105 by a later scholarly study:

```
et pedes eius similes aurocalco sicut de fornace igneo
et uox eius ut sonus aquarum multarum
```

This gives us a useful provisional reading, but it does not remove the need to inspect the printed page and manuscript.

### Comparison with the Clementine Vulgate

|Feature|Fleury – Buchanan OCR / reported reading|Clementine Vulgate|
|---|---|---|
|Head and hair|`caput autem eius et capilli`|`caput autem ejus, et capilli`|
|Verb|`erat` in OCR; probably `erant`|`erant`|
|Wool|`uelut lana` (damaged/restored)|`tamquam lana alba`|
|Snow|`aut nix`|`et tamquam nix`|
|Eyes|`ut flamma ignis`|`tamquam flamma ignis`|
|Feet|`similes aurocalco`|`similes auricalco`|
|Furnace|`sicut de fornace igneo`|`sicut in camino ardenti`|
|Voice|`ut sonus aquarum multarum`|`tamquam vox aquarum multarum`|

The Fleury wording is not simply an earlier copy of the familiar Vulgate. The differences in the wool/snow construction, the furnace phrase and the voice comparison are potentially significant.

The Vulgate column is a comparison text only. It must not be used to fill gaps in the Fleury transcription.

## Context

The Fleury Palimpsest is an important surviving witness to an early Latin text of Revelation. Its underlying biblical manuscript is conventionally dated to the fifth century. It preserves Revelation 1:1–2:1, and therefore includes Revelation 1:14–15 in the surviving portion.

The text of Revelation and Acts is classified in the modern _Catalogue of Latin New Testament Manuscripts_ as an **early African form**, slightly postdating Cyprian. This is a description of the textual tradition, not proof that the physical manuscript was copied in Africa.

The manuscript itself is considerably later than the origins of that translation tradition.

The codex was subsequently erased and reused as a palimpsest in France, probably in the seventh or eighth century. Its later association with the Benedictine abbey of Fleury accounts for its conventional name.

This is an Old Latin witness, not a manuscript of the standard Vulgate text.

### Provenance qualification

The modern _Catalogue of Latin New Testament Manuscripts_ gives Italy as the probable place of copying. An older attribution in _Codices Latini Antiquiores_ favoured Africa. The NUI Galway manuscript record notes that the attribution was subsequently changed to follow the Italian provenance proposed in a later CLA volume.

Record the origin as **uncertain, with Italy the current catalogue preference**. The African character of the text must not be confused with the geographical origin of the physical codex.

### Textual classification and significance

H. A. G. Houghton, _The Latin New Testament_, catalogue entry VL 55, p. 235, describes the manuscript as follows in substance:

- It is the Fleury Palimpsest, containing portions of Acts, the Catholic Epistles and Revelation.
    
- It was copied in the fifth century, probably in Italy.
    
- It was palimpsested in France in the seventh or eighth century.
    
- Its Revelation and Acts text represents an early African form slightly postdating Cyprian.
    
- Its Catholic Epistles have a different textual character, immediately preceding the Vulgate.
    

This last distinction is important: **the different biblical sections of the same physical codex need not represent the same textual tradition.**

The Fleury manuscript is the earliest surviving continuous-text Latin manuscript of Revelation. That does not make it the earliest Latin evidence for the passage: earlier Latin quotations survive in Christian writers, particularly Irenaeus and Cyprian.

The Vulgate text of Revelation represents a later Latin textual form. Its relationship to Greek witnesses, including Codex Sinaiticus, should be investigated separately rather than assumed to explain the Fleury text.

The next collation should distinguish three levels:

1. **Manuscript evidence:** letters and words actually legible in the undertext.
    
2. **Editorial reconstruction:** letters or words supplied by Berger, Buchanan or later editors.
    
3. **Normalised reading:** a readable Latin text with spelling, punctuation and abbreviations expanded according to stated editorial policy.
    

The OCR text is useful as a search aid but is not an adequate basis for a diplomatic transcription. The actual readings of VL 55 at Revelation 1:14–15 must be checked against Buchanan's printed page and, where possible, the BnF manuscript images.

## Translations

### Provisional translation of the OCR-derived reading

> His head, and his hairs, were [white] like wool or snow; and his eyes were like a flame of fire; and his feet were like aurichalcum, as if from a fiery furnace; and his voice was like the sound of many waters.

This is a provisional translation of the reported Latin wording, not a translation of a verified diplomatic transcription.

The supplied word “[white]” is required by the sense and the parallel tradition, but the surviving OCR excerpt does not securely establish its exact position or form.

## Variants and editorial reconstructions

The most important provisional differences from the Clementine Vulgate are:

- `uelut` rather than `tamquam` in the wool comparison.
    
- `aut nix` rather than `et tamquam nix`.
    
- `ut flamma ignis` rather than `tamquam flamma ignis`.
    
- `aurocalco` rather than `auricalco`.
    
- `sicut de fornace igneo` rather than `sicut in camino ardenti`.
    
- `ut sonus aquarum multarum` rather than `tamquam vox aquarum multarum`.
    

These should not yet be treated as a fully verified apparatus. The printed edition and its corrections need to be checked.

The spelling `aurocalco` is especially worth retaining provisionally rather than silently normalising to `auricalco` or `orichalco`.

## Lexical and grammatical notes

|Latin expression|Meaning|Research relevance|
|---|---|---|
|`caput`|head|The description begins with the head, as in the Greek.|
|`capilli`|hairs|Refers to the hair; do not assume it means hair colour independently of the surrounding syntax.|
|`albus` / `candidus`|white; bright or shining white|The exact adjective in Fleury needs verification. These words are not automatically interchangeable in nuance.|
|`lana`|wool|The wool comparison is part of the whiteness imagery.|
|`nix`|snow|A second comparison, apparently introduced by `aut` in the provisional text.|
|`oculi`|eyes|The description does not specify human eye pigmentation.|
|`flamma ignis`|flame of fire|A visual comparison; not an explicit description of red or pink eyes.|
|`aurocalcum`|aurichalcum / orichalcum|A special metallic term; not simply an unambiguous equivalent of ordinary bronze.|
|`fornax`|furnace, kiln|The Fleury phrase appears to differ from the Vulgate's `caminus`.|
|`igneus` / `ardens`|fiery / burning|The exact form and grammatical construction in Fleury require checking.|
|`uox`|voice|The OCR's `uos` is almost certainly an error.|

The phrase `sicut de fornace igneo` is grammatically unusual if `igneo` is intended to agree directly with feminine ablative `fornace`. This is another reason to inspect the printed page rather than emend it automatically to `ignea`.

## Reception and commentary

The Fleury manuscript is a witness to the Latin biblical text, not itself an exegetical commentary.

The African Latin textual tradition is represented earlier by Cyprian's quotations. His _Testimonia_ II.26 gives a direct quotation of Revelation 1:14–15 and is therefore an important point of comparison for the Fleury wording. The precise relationship between the quotation and the manuscript must be considered passage by passage.

The commentary of Primasius, bishop of Hadrumetum, who died around 560, is an important later witness to Latin interpretation. His biblical text and exposition should be compared with Cyprian and Fleury, but shared wording alone does not establish direct dependence.

Bede's _Explanatio Apocalypseos_, composed in the early eighth century, is a later British witness to the interpretation of the passage. His exposition should be compared with Primasius and earlier Latin exegesis before being treated as an independent interpretation.

The later production of Codex Amiatinus at Wearmouth–Jarrow is relevant to the availability of the Latin biblical text in England, but it is not evidence that the Fleury manuscript itself travelled to Britain.

## Interpretative questions

1. Does the African Latin text preserve the same sequence of head, hair, wool, snow and eyes as the Greek witnesses?
    
2. Does it use `albus`, `candidus`, or another term for the whiteness of the hair?
    
3. Does the Latin syntax make the whiteness apply to both head and hair, or is there a meaningful difference?
    
4. Does the Latin rendering of the eyes preserve the Greek flame-of-fire metaphor without modification?
    
5. What is the precise Latin equivalent of χαλκολίβανον?
    
6. Does the furnace phrase describe the feet as heated, glowing, or simply fiery?
    
7. Which readings are shared with Cyprian, and which distinguish the Fleury text?
    
8. Which readings are shared with Primasius, and what is the direction of textual or exegetical dependence?
    
9. Can any of these Latin readings be traced into Bede's biblical lemma or commentary?
    
10. Can a Latin form of the passage be demonstrated in Britain before the production of Amiatinus?
    

## Claims supported

- VL 55 is a fifth-century Latin manuscript witness to Revelation 1:14–15.
    
- The manuscript preserves Revelation 1:1–2:1.
    
- Its Revelation and Acts text represents an early African form of the Old Latin tradition, slightly postdating Cyprian.
    
- The underlying codex was reused as a palimpsest in France in the seventh or eighth century.
    
- The manuscript is held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France as Latin 6400 G.
    
- The manuscript is the earliest surviving continuous-text Latin manuscript of Revelation.
    
- Berger's 1889 edition and Buchanan's 1907 edition are principal published sources for its text.
    
- Buchanan's edition, as represented in searchable OCR and subsequent scholarly citation, gives a provisional reading of the passage that differs in several respects from the Clementine Vulgate.
    

## Uncertainties

- The precise place of origin remains disputed: Italy is the current catalogue preference, while an earlier catalogue attributed it probably to Africa.
    
- The exact date within the fifth century is not established here.
    
- The OCR-derived wording of Revelation 1:14–15 has not yet been checked against the printed page.
    
- It is not yet established which words are directly legible in the undertext and which depend on editorial reconstruction.
    
- The apparent reading `capilli erat` is probably an OCR error for `capilli erant`, but the printed edition needs checking.
    
- The OCR's `uos` is almost certainly `uox`, but the printed edition needs checking.
    
- The reading `igneo` requires verification.
    
- The exact relationship between the Fleury text and Cyprian's quotation at Revelation 1:14–15 requires passage-specific investigation.
    
- The exact textual relationship between Fleury and Primasius at Revelation 1:14–15 remains to be established.
    
- The presence of this Latin text in Britain before Bede must be demonstrated separately; a fifth-century manuscript of probable Italian origin does not establish British circulation.
