### **Lines 445–454 — The Nativity**

> After he chose Mary, the glory of maidens,\
> the renowned maiden, for his marriage-bond,\
> so that there, clothed in white garments,\
> angels did not appear when the prince came,\
> the man in Bethlehem. The messengers were ready,\
> who through a spoken song made known to the shepherds,\
> proclaimed the true joy, that a son had been\
> born in Middle-earth, the Lord's,\
> in Bethlehem. Nevertheless, the books do not say\
> that they appeared there in white garments\
> at that noble time, as they did afterwards.

A few qualifications:

- *mægða weolman* is an honorific description of Mary: something like the glory, ornament or pride of maidens.
- *mundheals* is difficult to render precisely. It belongs to the vocabulary of marriage, protection and marital commitment. "Marriage-bond" is a cautious rendering rather than a certain lexical equivalent.
- *hrægl* means garment or clothing.
- *oðeowdun* means appeared, manifested or showed themselves.

The important distinction is that the poet explicitly says the angels did **not** appear in white garments at the Nativity, according to the written accounts, whereas they did so on a later occasion.

### **Lines 545–549 — The Ascension**

> In heaven's keeping. White came\
> the angels, the benefactors of men, to meet him.\
> That is well spoken, as the writings say,\
> that all-bright angels came to meet him,\
> in companies, at that holy time,

The continuation is:

> to descend in the heavens. Then the greatest of feasts\
> had taken place in glory.

Here the two expressions are:

- *hwīte* — white (agreeing with the angels).
- *albeorhte* — wholly/all-bright, completely radiant.

Notice that the poet first says *hwīte* and then effectively restates the description as *albeorhte*. The second expression adds the idea of radiance, but it does not erase the first.

### **Lines 895–899 — The Last Judgement**

> There the entire host of angels and devils\
> shall be mingled together, brighter and pale/dark.\
> There shall be the coming of both,\
> the white and the black, as their dwelling-places\
> have been appointed differently, for angels and devils.\
> Then suddenly upon Mount Sion...

The relevant vocabulary:

- *beorhtra* — brighter, more brilliant.
- *blācra* — pale, shining, or possibly livid, depending on context.
- *hwitra* — whiter.
- *sweartra* — blacker, darker.

There is a distinction worth preserving here. The poet uses *blāc* in the first comparison and *swart* in the second. They are not simply repetitions of the same word.

### **Lines 1015–1019 — The nature of humanity and angels**

> Therefore it is no wonder that the impure nature\
> of worldly men, grieving with cares,\
> fears them greatly, when the holy nature,\
> white and heaven-bright, the host of high angels,\
> are terrified before that appearance,

The grammatical structure is important:

- *seo unclæne gecynd* — the unclean nature.
- *sio halge gecynd* — the holy nature.
- *hwit ond heofonbeorht* — white and heaven-bright.
- *heagengla mægen* — the might/host of high angels.

The whiteness here is explicitly associated with holiness and the angelic nature. *Heofonbeorht* is stronger than merely "bright": literally, heaven-bright.

### **Lines 1110–1114 — Christ's wounds**

> The white hands and the holy feet,\
> and likewise blood flowed from his side,\
> where blood and water, both together,\
> came out, before the sight of eyes,\
> to run before men, when he was on the cross.

The key expressions:

- *hwitan honda* — white hands.
- *halgan fet* — holy feet.
- *swat* — blood.
- *blod ond wæter* — blood and water.
- *fore eagna gesyhð* — before the sight of eyes.

The poet's juxtaposition of *hwitan* and *halgan* is worth retaining. The hands are described as white; the feet as holy. They are not grammatically the same description.

## **Some observations about the language**

For your research into extraordinary whiteness, I would particularly preserve the following distinctions rather than normalising them in translation.

| Old English    | Literal sense                            | Occurrence                              |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| *hwīt*         | white                                    | Angels, garments, hands, angels' nature |
| *albeorht*     | wholly bright, all-radiant               | Angels at the Ascension                 |
| *heofonbeorht* | heaven-bright                            | Holy nature of the angels               |
| *beorht*       | bright, shining                          | Angels and other heavenly beings        |
| *blāc*         | pale, shining, livid (context-dependent) | Angels and devils                       |
| *swart*        | black, dark                              | Devils                                  |

Three points particularly interest me.

**1. White clothing is not simply assumed at the Nativity.**

The poet explicitly distinguishes the Nativity from the Ascension, and appeals to the written accounts as authority. This is evidence of conscious reflection on the visual representation of angels, rather than simply an automatic association between angels and white clothing.

**2. Whiteness and brightness are related but not interchangeable.**

At the Ascension, *hwīte* and *albeorhte* occur in successive lines. At the Last Judgement, *hwītra* and *sweartra* are contrasted, while *beorhtra* and *blācra* provide another distinction.

These lexical choices deserve separate annotation in your corpus.

**3. The description of Christ's hands is unusually concrete.**

*Þa hwitan honda* — the white hands.

This is not simply a description of heavenly light or an abstract quality of holiness. It is a physical description of Christ's body, immediately followed by the blood and water issuing from his side.

That makes it particularly interesting alongside your Daniel and Revelation material.

## **One caution about the translation**

Do not treat *blāc* as a straightforward synonym for either white or black. Its semantic range includes pale and shining, with potentially rather different connotations. The contrast with *sweartra* in the following line is important, but does not settle its precise colour value.

Likewise, I would leave *mundheals* flagged for further lexical investigation rather than build an argument on my provisional translation.
