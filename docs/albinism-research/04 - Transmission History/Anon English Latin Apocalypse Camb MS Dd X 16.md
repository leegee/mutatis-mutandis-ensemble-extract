[In The Bible And The Apocrypha In The Early Irish Church](https://dokumen.pub/the-bible-and-the-apocrypha-in-the-early-irish-church-ad-600-1200-instrumenta-patristica-et-mediaevalia-66-9782503547954-2503547958.html), Martin McNamara discusses an anonymous commentary on Revelation preserved in **Cambridge University Library MS Dd X 16**. The manuscript is probably ninth- or tenth-century, and was likely copied in Brittany. Its script and abbreviations show strong Insular and Irish influence.

More importantly, Roger Gryson considers this commentary and the related _De enigmatibus_ to depend on an earlier commentary written in Ireland in the early eighth century. McNamara regards the precise place of composition as unresolved: Ireland, Britain or the Continent remain possibilities.

## **Head and hair**

It begins:

> _caput eius. id est praepossiti. capilli. Id est subiecti ..._
> His head: that is, the superiors. His hairs: that is, the subordinates ...

It then explains that the head and hair together signify all the saints. An alternative interpretation identifies the hairs as:

> _multitudo alborum in fide_
> the multitude of those who are white in faith.

The wool comparison is explained through the sheep:

> _propter infirmitatem et innocentiam et humilitatem_
> because of weakness, innocence and humility.

The snow comparison refers to:

> _innumerabilem turbam candidorum a celo datorum_
>the innumerable multitude of the white ones given from heaven.

They are identified with the heavenly Jerusalem, and the gloss adds the rather striking phrase:

> _propter frigus castitatis_
> because of the coldness of chastity.

This is a different elaboration from Bede's. The white hair becomes a hierarchy of believers; wool becomes an image of humility and innocence; snow becomes the multitude of the elect and the heavenly Jerusalem. The additional association with chastity is especially worth noting.

## **Eyes**

The gloss reads:

> _et oculi eius uelut flamma ignis. id est spiritus septiformis uel precepta diuina._
> And his eyes were like a flame of fire: that is, the sevenfold Spirit or divine commandments.

It supports this interpretation with two Psalms:

- Psalm 118:105 (modern numbering 119:105): _Lucerna pedibus meis verbum tuum_ — "Your word is a lamp to my feet."
    
- Psalm 104:19 (modern numbering 105:19): _Eloquium Domini inflammauit eum_ — "The word of the Lord inflamed him."
    
The fiery eyes are therefore understood as divine illumination and instruction, rather than as a description of eye colour.

## **Feet**

The gloss reads:

> _Pedes autem igniti aeclesiam nouissimi temporis nimietate praesurarum ignitam indicat._
> The fiery feet indicate the Church of the last time, made fiery by the intensity of its persecutions.

It adds that feet are the last part of the body. The imagery is thus interpreted as the Church's final period of suffering and persecution.

This is very close to Bede's interpretation of the feet, although we should not yet assume direct borrowing in either direction.
