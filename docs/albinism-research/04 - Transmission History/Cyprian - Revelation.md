## Date

Conventionally around 248-250 CE. The work is associated with Carthage. The precise chronology of its constituent books and chapters is not entirely settled; Book III may have been added later than Books I-II.
## Context

Cyprian of Carthage (d. 258), bishop of Carthage in Roman North Africa, compiled a collection of biblical _testimonia_ — scriptural passages arranged under thematic headings — for the use of Christians in explaining and defending their beliefs.

_Ad Quirinum_ is arranged in three books. The first addresses claims concerning Judaism; the second assembles scriptural evidence for Christian beliefs about Christ; the third concerns moral instruction. Book II, chapter 26 belongs to the Christological section.

The chapter heading reads:

> QVOD CVM RESVRREXISSET, ACCIPERET A PATRE OMNEM POTESTATEM ET POTESTAS EIVS AETERNA SIT.

“That, when he had risen, he would receive all power from the Father, and that his power is eternal.”

The chapter assembles passages from Daniel 7:13-14, Isaiah 33:10-12, Psalm 110:1-2, Revelation 1:12-18 and Matthew 28:18-20. Revelation is introduced by the formula _Item in Apocalypsi_ (“Likewise, in the Apocalypse”).

The quotation is therefore part of an argument about Christ's resurrection, authority and eternal sovereignty. Cyprian is not offering an independent description of Christ's physical appearance, nor interpreting the significance of the whiteness or the fiery eyes in this chapter.


## Witnesses

It is essential to distinguish the date of the work from the dates of the surviving manuscripts that transmit it.

|Witness or evidence|Date|Significance|
|---|---|---|
|Cyprian's _Ad Quirinum_|c.248-250|Date of the literary quotation; not the date of an extant manuscript of Revelation|
|Manuscript tradition of _Ad Quirinum_|Later witnesses|Transmits Cyprian's Latin text, subject to variation and subsequent adjustment of biblical quotations|
|Hartel edition, CSEL 3.1|1868|Printed critical text used for the transcription below|
|Weber edition, CCSL 3|1972|Modern critical edition, with a critical apparatus|

The quotation is valuable because it provides a securely datable example of Revelation 1:14-15 circulating in Latin in the middle of the third century. It is not, however, a surviving third-century copy of Revelation itself.

The distinction matters when comparing Cyprian with the fifth-century Fleury Palimpsest (VL 55). Cyprian supplies early literary evidence for a Latin form of the passage; Fleury supplies physical manuscript evidence for a Latin copy of Revelation.

The form _poderem_ in the longer quotation is retained as printed in the transcription consulted; it should be checked against the critical edition before being used in a detailed study of the clothing terminology.

## Translations

### Machine Translation of Revelation 1:14-15 as quoted by Cyprian

> His head and his hair were white, like wool or snow; and his eyes were like a flame of fire; and his feet were like aurichalcum, as if from a fiery furnace; and his voice was like the sound of many waters.

### Translation notes

>_caput autem eius et capilli erant albi_
  his head and his hair were white
 
 The adjective _albi_ is plural masculine, agreeing with the coordinated _caput ... et capilli_ in the wording as transmitted.
    
> *uelut lana aut nix*
>  like wool or snow
  
The alternatives are joined by _aut_, rather than by the repeated comparison found in some later Latin forms.
    
> _oculi eius ut flamma ignis_
> his eyes were like a flame of fire
    
> _pedes eius similes aurichalco_
> his feet were like aurichalcum
    
> _sicut de fornace ignea_
>as if from a fiery furnace

The wording is not identical to the later Vulgate's _sicut in camino ardenti_.
    
>_uox eius ut sonus aquarum multarum_
> his voice was like the sound of many waters

Several observations follow, with different degrees of certainty:

1. **The plural** _**erant**_**.** Cyprian's text has _capilli erant_. This is relevant to the Fleury OCR's _capilli erat_, which may be an OCR error or a misreading of damaged writing. Cyprian's wording is useful comparative evidence, but cannot by itself establish what the Fleury manuscript reads.
    
2. **The comparison with wool and snow.** Cyprian has _uelut lana aut nix_: “like wool or snow”. The Clementine Vulgate has two coordinated comparisons, _tamquam lana alba ... et tamquam nix_. The difference is not merely spelling; the syntax and presentation of the comparison differ.
    
3. **The feet and furnace.** Cyprian's _sicut de fornace ignea_ (“as if from a fiery furnace”) differs from the Vulgate's _sicut in camino ardenti_ (“as in a burning furnace”). The preposition and the adjective are both worth tracking across witnesses.
    
4. **Aurichalcum.** Hartel's text has _aurichalco_. The precise spelling and form should be checked against Weber's apparatus before being used to establish a stemmatic relationship with Fleury's apparent _aurocalco_.
    
5. **Textual transmission of** _**Ad Quirinum**_**.** The biblical quotations in the work were subject to adjustment during transmission. A collation of witnesses identifies at least five strands in the textual tradition, reflecting conformity to differing Latin biblical texts. Consequently, the wording in a modern edition should not automatically be treated as an untouched record of Cyprian's original biblical exemplar.
    

The comparison with Fleury is therefore suggestive, but it does not yet demonstrate direct textual descent from Cyprian's biblical text to the Fleury manuscript, or vice versa.

## Reception and commentary

The chapter's heading supplies the interpretative context: Christ's resurrection, the authority received from the Father, and the eternity of that authority.

Cyprian places Revelation 1:12-18 alongside other scriptural passages that support this theological claim. The description of Christ is therefore functioning as scriptural testimony to his exalted status, rather than as a passage for the interpretation of physical difference.

This is important when tracing the subsequent reception of the imagery. The quotation makes the white head and hair, fiery eyes and furnace-associated feet available to Latin readers, but the chapter does not itself explain those details as symbols of age, purity, divine knowledge, judgement or any human physical condition.

**Cyprian's text also belongs to the Latin biblical tradition of North Africa**. It is valuable evidence for the form of the passage circulating in that environment by the middle of the third century. It should not, however, be treated as a direct transcription of a single recoverable Latin Bible manuscript.

**Edwina Murphy**'s study of the compilation of _Ad Quirinum_ is relevant here. She examines the role of memory and inherited tradition in Cyprian's scriptural citations, and argues that variations between _Ad Quirinum_ and Cyprian's other works do not in themselves disprove his authorship. The question of compilation and the relationship between citation, memory and written biblical texts remains significant when evaluating the exact textual authority of this passage.

## Interpretative questions

1. **What Latin biblical text did Cyprian use?**  
    Does the wording of Revelation 1:14-15 in _Ad Quirinum_ reflect a stable North African Latin version, a text adapted by Cyprian, or a combination of written biblical text and remembered citation?
    
2. **How stable is the wording in the manuscript tradition?**  
    Which readings in Hartel and Weber are supported by the manuscript witnesses, and which may reflect later conformity to other Latin biblical forms?
    
3. **How does Cyprian relate to Fleury?**  
    The similarities in _caput ... et capilli_, _lana aut nix_, the fiery eyes, aurichalcum and the furnace are worth investigating. They do not yet establish direct dependence or a common immediate exemplar.
    
4. **What is the significance of** _**albi**_**?**  
    The adjective explicitly describes the head and hair as white. Does the difference between _albi_ here and _candidi_ in later Latin forms reflect stylistic variation, textual tradition or a more significant semantic distinction?
    
5. **How should the imagery be separated analytically?**  
    The text places whiteness, fire and metal imagery together, but does not explain them as a single physical or symbolic phenomenon. Later interpreters may connect them, but that connection should be recorded as reception rather than read back into Cyprian's wording.
    
6. **How much of the quotation can be attributed to Cyprian personally?**  
    The work is traditionally attributed to him, but the history of its compilation and the possibility of inherited collections or remembered citations should be considered.

## Uncertainties

- **The exact date:** c.248-250 is the conventional working range, but the dating of individual books and stages of compilation is not completely settled.
    
- **Authorship and compilation:** the traditional attribution is to Cyprian, but modern scholarship continues to discuss the process by which _Ad Quirinum_ was assembled.
    
- **The original biblical exemplar:** the wording in the edited text does not by itself identify the exact Latin manuscript or textual form used by Cyprian.
    
- **The manuscript variants:** the five textual strands identified in the transmission of _Ad Quirinum_ demonstrate that the scriptural citations were subject to change. A full collation of II.26 is still needed.
    
- **The exact status of** _**aurichalco**_ **and** _**fornace ignea**_**:** these readings should be checked in Weber's apparatus before making detailed claims about their antiquity or relationship to Fleury.
    
- **The Fleury comparison:** the OCR transcription is provisional. The manuscript image and Buchanan's printed transcription need to be checked before the comparison can be treated as a reliable collation.
    
- **The relationship to the Greek text:** Cyprian is evidence for an early Latin form of Revelation, not direct evidence for the precise Greek wording of his exemplar.
    
- **Theological interpretation:** this chapter does not explain the symbolism of the white hair or fiery eyes. Any such interpretation must be sourced separately.

### **What is especially significant?**

#### **A. The whiteness clause is unusually stable**

The apparatus does not report a variant for:

```
capilli erant albi
```

The main text and the cited witnesses therefore support the plural `erant albi` strongly. This is useful for our comparison with Fleury's OCR `capilli erat`, which is likely an OCR or transcription problem rather than a genuine reading.

However, absence of a variant in this apparatus is not proof that every manuscript agrees: it means Weber does not record a variant at this point.

#### **B. Wool becomes explicitly white in some witnesses**

Two witnesses add `alba` after `lana`:

```
lana alba aut nix
```

Weber's main text instead has:

```
lana aut nix
```

This is a small but revealing alteration. A scribe could make the wool explicitly white, even though `albi` already describes the head and hair. It is worth recording as a possible clarification of the imagery, not as evidence of a fundamentally different interpretation.

#### **C. The aurichalcum spelling is not settled**

Weber prints:

```
aurochalco
```

but records:

```
aurichalco
```

in a substantial group of witnesses. The difference is orthographic/lexical-form variation around the same metal term; it does not change the imagery from metallic to ordinary bodily colour.

For your corpus, I would normalise both forms to a lexical entry such as `aurichalcum`, but preserve the actual witness spelling in the transcription.

#### **D. The furnace phrase is the most interesting local variation**

Weber prints:

```
sicut de fornace ignea
```

The apparatus records:

- `igneo` — V, R
    
- `igne` — O*, B
    
- `ignis` — W, D
    
- `+ ardentis` — D
    

The last addition produces an expanded form equivalent to _from a fiery, burning furnace_. The variation may reflect grammatical adjustment or clarification of the fire imagery. It does not, however, change the basic image of feet associated with a furnace.
