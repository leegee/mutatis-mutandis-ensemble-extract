# Isaiah 1:18 — Scarlet made white

## Context

Isaiah 1:18 belongs to the prophet’s indictment and appeal in Isaiah 1:2–20. The verse uses the colours of scarlet/crimson and white snow/wool in a conditional image concerning Israel’s sins and their cleansing or transformation. It is a strong colour contrast, but it describes neither a human body nor complexion.

## Witnesses

This working note follows the pointed Masoretic text as represented by the Westminster Leningrad Codex (WLC). No full manuscript or versional collation is attempted.

## Transcriptions

**Masoretic Hebrew (WLC):**

> לְכוּ־נָא וְנִוָּכְחָה יֹאמַר יְהוָה אִם־יִהְיוּ חֲטָאֵיכֶם כַּשָּׁנִים כַּשֶּׁלֶג יַלְבִּינוּ אִם־יַאְדִּימוּ כַתּוֹלָע כַּצֶּמֶר יִהְיוּ׃

## Translations

**Close working translation:**

> Come, let us reason together, says The LORD: though your sins are like scarlet, they shall become white as snow; though they are red like crimson, they shall be like wool.

The verse presents two parallel red-to-white comparisons: scarlet becomes snow-white, and crimson-red becomes wool-like. The language is metaphorical and addressed to the people collectively through their sins.

## Variants and editorial reconstructions

No textual variant or editorial reconstruction is adopted here. Translation details vary, especially in the force of the invitation “let us reason/settle matters” and the precise terms for the dyes.

The אדם-root form here is a Hifil verb, not the Qal verb אָדְמוּ in Lamentations 4:7 or the adjective אָדֹם in Song of Songs 5:10. Its presence in a red-and-white contrast is relevant to the project’s colour-language comparisons, but the symbolic referent changes the evidentiary value.

## Reception and commentary

Isaiah 1:18 became an influential scriptural image of cleansing, forgiveness, and transformation. Later interpreters may connect its snow-and-wool whiteness with other white imagery, but such reception should be distinguished from the verse’s own metaphorical context.

## Interpretative questions

- Do scarlet and crimson refer to dye, stained cloth, or an intentionally overlapping pair of red images?
- Does the movement from red to white primarily signify forgiveness, purification, or a broader reversal of guilt and consequence?
- How can Isaiah serve as a contrast for bodily red/white descriptions without implying the passages share the same sense?

## Claims supported

- Isaiah 1:18 is a prominent biblical red/white contrast: scarlet/crimson is paired with snow/wool whiteness.
- The passage is symbolic and ethical, not a description of complexion.
- אדם-root red language participates in a different grammatical and semantic setting here than in Song of Songs 5:10 and Lamentations 4:7.

## Uncertainties

- Exact English equivalents for the dye terms remain debated.
- The rhetoric of the conditional clauses and the nature of the promised transformation are interpreted differently.
