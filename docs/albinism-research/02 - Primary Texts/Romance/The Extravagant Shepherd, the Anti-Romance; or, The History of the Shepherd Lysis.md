# Anselme's Portrait of Charite: Snow-White Complexion, Solar Eyes and Golden Hair

## Context

In Charles Sorel's *Le Berger extravagant* (1627–28), translated into English by John Davies in 1653, the shepherd Lysis encounters a painted representation of Charite. Anselme has constructed the portrait from the elaborate poetic descriptions of beauty that Lysis has given him.

Lysis initially fails to recognise the image as a human face: he sees instead an assemblage of chains, suns and flowers. The portrait literalises the metaphors of pastoral and courtly love poetry, turning conventional descriptions of the beloved into an extravagant visual composition.

The episode belongs to Sorel's satirical attack on the artificiality and excesses of romance conventions. Its value as historical evidence lies in the recognisability of the visual vocabulary being parodied.

## Transcriptions

Selected wording from the English passage:

> he had painted a Face, which instead of being of a flesh-colour, was of a complexion white as snow;

Other distinctive phrases include:

> two Suns sending forth beams

The eyes are further associated with "flames" and "darts". The eyebrows are described as "“"black as Ebony".

The portrait also includes a mouth represented by branches of **coral**, cheeks formed from lilies and roses, a forehead smooth as ice, and golden hair transformed into chains, networks and baited hooks.

## Lexical and grammatical notes

- **“Complexion white as snow”**: An explicit description of the skin's colour, rather than the whiteness of hair or clothing. The comparison with snow intensifies the whiteness and distinguishes it from ordinary flesh-colour.
    
- **“Flesh-colour”**: The contrast is significant. The painted face is said to depart from the expected colour of human flesh.
    
- **“Two Suns”**: The eyes are not merely bright; they are represented as independent sources of solar radiance.
    
- **“Beams”, “flames” and “darts”**: Radiance becomes an active force. The eyes emit light and also inflict erotic injury.
    
- **“Black as Ebony”**: The eyebrows provide a sharply contrasting dark element against the white complexion.
    
- **“Lily” and “rose”**: The cheeks combine white and red floral imagery, reproducing the conventional contrast of pale and red complexion.
    
- **“Smooth as ice”**: Extends the imagery of whiteness and coldness to the forehead.
    
- **Golden chains and networks**: The hair becomes an elaborate mechanism for ensnaring lovers.
    

## Reception and commentary

The passage satirises the conventions of idealised beauty in pastoral and courtly romance. Its humour depends upon the literalisation of poetic metaphors: the beloved's eyes really become suns, her cheeks flowers, and her hair a network of golden snares.

There are suggestive correspondences with Revelation 1:14–16:

- The figure's hair is “white as snow”.    
- His eyes are “like a flame of fire”.    
- His face shines like the sun.

Sorel's portrait relocates and recombines these attributes: snow-whiteness is applied to the complexion, solar radiance to the eyes, and fire becomes an attribute of their erotic power.

## Interpretative questions

1. Does the French original use the same snow-white complexion, or is this a feature of Davies's translation?
    
2. How early can the combination of snow-white skin, solar eyes and fiery radiance be traced in English writing?
    
3. To what extent does the description draw on biblical and apocalyptic imagery, particularly Daniel 7, Daniel 10 and Revelation 1?
    
4. How does this imagery interact with the classical white-and-red ideal of female beauty?
    
5. When does the language of supernatural radiance become conventional in descriptions of human beauty?
    
6. Does the satirical treatment depend on the recognisability of imagery originally associated with divine, angelic or otherwise extraordinary beings?
    
7. How does the description relate to the history of representations of unusually pale human bodies, without necessarily describing albinism?
    

## Claims supported

- By the mid-seventeenth century, snow-white complexion, solar eyes, fiery radiance and contrasting colours could be combined within a recognisable literary vocabulary of idealised beauty.
    
- The vocabulary was sufficiently familiar to be exaggerated and satirised.
    
- Whiteness of complexion could be represented as a departure from ordinary flesh-colour and as part of an extravagant ideal of beauty.
    
- Biblical, apocalyptic, classical and courtly visual conventions offer relevant comparative traditions for investigating the emergence of this vocabulary.
    
- The passage demonstrates the migration and recombination of imagery across different representational contexts; it does not by itself establish the direction or precise route of transmission.
    

## Uncertainties

- Correspondence between the English translation and the French original.
- Whether the imagery reflects a specific biblical allusion or a broader shared visual repertoire.
- The relative contributions of biblical, classical and courtly traditions to the conventions being parodied.
- The extent to which the portrait's extraordinary whiteness would have been understood as supernatural, idealised, erotic or simply conventional.
