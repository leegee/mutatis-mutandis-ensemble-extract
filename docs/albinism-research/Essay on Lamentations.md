# Distinguished by Appearance: Reconsidering Salters' Lamentations 4:7-8

## Abstract

Where Lamentations 4:7 describes the former appearance of Jerusalem's _nəzîrêhā_ in terms of snow, milk, a red substance and sapphire, the sense of nearly every term is disputed. This essay examines how R. B. Salters' 2010 ICC commentary resolves those disputes as a description of the 'refined beauty of ... nobles' (p. 209). It reconstructs his philological reasoning and the history of interpretation behind it, and argues that his translation is coherent but not required by the Hebrew. In particular, the traditional identification as 'Nazirites' remains lexically viable, and the Hebrew provides no clear lexical basis for preferring the hair reading of גִּזְרָתָם (_gizratām_) over alternatives. The essay then asks what verses 1-8 imply about the function of the description, and suggests that the poem's framing of the loss in verse 8 is compatible with the colours having made the subjects publicly identifiable, a function not fully explained by an ideal of beauty. The argument is tentative: rather than proposing a new translation, the essay shows how assumptions about aesthetic ideals may have shaped the interpretation of a physical description whose precise meaning remains uncertain.
## Introduction: an unresolved physical description in Lamentations 4:7

The earliest English surviving complete renditions of Lamentations are the Wycliffite versions of the closing decades of the fourteenth century.[^1] The passage offers a striking description of an exceptional group:

> Whittere is Nazareis þan ſnoȝ, ſhynendere þan mylc; reddere þan old yuer, þan ſafyr fairere.

— Early Wycliffe

> Nazareis therof weren whitere than snow, schynyngere than mylk; rodier than elde yuer, fairere than safire.

— Late Wycliffe

> Her Nazarites were purer then snow, they were whiter then milke, they were more ruddie in body then rubies, their polishing was of Saphir.

— King James Version

> Her nobles had been brighter than snow, more dazzling than milk; in body they were ruddier than coral, their hair (shone) like sapphire.

— Salters (2010)

Considered individually, each version presents an unusual physical description. Considered together, their differences suggest a significant interpretative problem. These are not simply alternative choices of English vocabulary, but reflect different understandings of the underlying Hebrew, developed through distinct traditions of translation and interpretation.

This essay examines the interpretative reasoning underlying the translation of this passage in R. B. Salters' _Lamentations: A Critical and Exegetical Commentary_ (2010), published in the International Critical Commentary series. I draw on Salters' extensive philological and textual analysis, as well as the earlier scholarship on which it builds. His treatment of 4:7-8 is particularly valuable for its detailed consideration of the disputed Hebrew expressions and the history of their interpretation.

I argue three related points. First, Salters' rendering of נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_) as 'nobles' rests on the sense of being 'set apart', a meaning that applies at least as directly to the traditional identification of the subjects as Nazirites. Second, I shall show that the evidence for interpreting _gizratām_ as hair is no stronger than that supporting the proposed references to veins or the cutting of precious stones, and that Salters' preference for hair is easier to understand once the passage has been read as a celebration of refined physical beauty. Third, the treatment of the subjects' appearance in verse 8 is compatible with their colouring having been a means of recognising them. This does not decide between 'nobles' and 'Nazirites', but it does shift the interpretative emphasis away from an assumed ideal of beauty towards the possibility of a visually distinctive appearance. I do not propose a replacement translation; rather, I aim to show where the existing one depends on interpretative choices that the Hebrew does not compel and that the text remains open to alternative readings.
## The history of interpretation

### Defining the subject: Jerusalem's Nazirites, or not?

The subject of the passage is indicated by נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_), traditionally translated 'her Nazirites' in English.[^2] The word derives from נָזִיר (_nāzîr_), related to the root נזר (_n-z-r_), meaning to separate or set apart. Its familiar biblical usage is for someone consecrated to God, particularly a person undertaking the Nazirite vow described in Numbers 6:1-21, which includes abstention from wine, avoidance of contact with the dead and the prohibition against cutting one's hair. This traditional interpretation is supported by the Septuagint, Peshitta, Vulgate, Targum and Symmachus, as well as Midrash Rabbah, Rashi, Calvin and a number of modern commentators.

Several alternatives have been proposed. Some consider the Masoretic reading to be the result of a scribal error and emend the word to נְעָרֶיהָ (_nəʿārêhā_), 'her young people' or 'her youths'. Kara (c. 1050-1125) reads 'her hair' by analogy with נִזְרֵךְ (_nizrēḵ_) in Jeremiah 7:29, to make the passage a description of hair becoming white through illness. Salters notes this reading requires זַכּוּ (_zakkû_) to be understood as a declarative perfect describing a present rather than a past condition. As such it sits less comfortably with the chapter's recurring contrast between former glory and present degradation.

Luther follows _Midrash Leqah Tob_ to derive the sense 'princes' (שָׂרֶיהָ) from the idea of being set apart or distinguished, via Nahum 3:17.

Salters draws on Albrektson and Renkema[^3] in developing a broader, non-technical understanding of _nāzîr_ as someone distinguished or pre-eminent. Albrektson cites Genesis 49:26 and Deuteronomy 33:16, where Joseph is called the _nāzîr_ of his brothers. The term may mean that he is distinguished or set apart from them, but these passages do not establish that it denotes a social rank. Renkema argues that the poem could not appropriately use the ordinary Hebrew term for 'princes,' שָׂרִ֗ים (_śārîm_), since the fate of Jerusalem's rulers is recounted earlier in the poem, and therefore proposes the less specific 'nobles'. Salters questions the dependence of this argument on the unity of the book, but nonetheless adopts 'nobles' in his translation, although the broader sense of _nāzîr_ as someone distinguished or set apart does not in itself establish social rank.
### White

There is little disagreement over the basic lexical meaning of זַכּוּ (_zakkû_), a form of זכה (_zākâ_), meaning 'to be clean, pure or bright'. The word can also carry a metaphorical sense of moral purity, as in Psalm 51:6 and Job 15:14 and 25:4, but Salters considers that unlikely here. The parallel verb צַחוּ (_ṣāḥû_), also associated with brightness or whiteness, and the subsequent references to redness and darkness suggest that both verbs describe physical appearance rather than moral qualities.

Salters follows Brenner in arguing that the comparisons with snow and milk give the verbs a specifically white sense, since both substances are used elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible as comparisons for whiteness. He rejects the possibility that the description refers to clothing, because 'the passage goes on to speak of the body', and so understands the comparisons as referring to the subjects' skin (p. 301):

> The skin of someone living in Palestine is likely to be a shade of olive, but it is possible that the paler the skin the more refined a person was thought to be.

He regards this, like all the descriptive language of the passage, as 'hyperbolic' (p. 299).
### Red

Salters notes that the second half of verse 7 is difficult. The Masoretic Text reads אָדְמוּ עֶצֶם מִפְּנִינִים (_ʾādmû ʿeṣem mippənînîm_), approximately 'they were redder in body than _penînîm_.' עֶצֶם (_ʿeṣem_), ordinarily 'bone' and by extension 'body' or 'substance', is singular where the plural verb might lead one to expect a plural or collective subject. Salters follows the KJV and other scholars in treating it as an adverbial accusative, 'in body', although he acknowledges (p. 301) Gottlieb's objection that this is forced. The ancient versions offer no clear resolution: the Septuagint omits the word, the Vulgate has 'old ivory', the Peshitta 'their bones', and the Targum and Rashi 'appearance'.

The verb אָדְמוּ (_ʾādmû_), 'they were red', is less disputed; the uncertainty lies chiefly in the comparison. Elsewhere _penînîm_ denotes something precious or valuable, never explicitly a colour; its precise identity is unknown, with coral, pearls and other precious stones proposed. Salters argues that the verb constrains the interpretation: whatever _penînîm_ denotes, it must be compatible with redness. He therefore reads the clause as describing a ruddy physical appearance, without claiming certainty about the material or the function of עֶצֶם.
### Blue

The final expression of the verse, סַפִּיר גִּזְרָתָם (_sappîr gizratām_), presents a further difficulty. Salters identifies גִּזְרָתָם (_gizratām_) as a rare word derived from גזר (_g-z-r_), meaning 'to cut' or 'divide', but notes that its precise referent is unclear. Its only other biblical occurrences, in Ezekiel 41-42, refer to a separated room or forecourt and offer little help. The ancient versions vary: the Septuagint has 'their pieces' or 'detached portion', Symmachus 'their limbs', the Peshitta 'their body', the RSV 'their form', Luther 'their appearance', and the Targum 'their faces'. Jerome's _pulchriores_, 'more fair', is a freer rendering.

One possibility is that the comparison refers to the subjects' form or shaping, with the סַפִּיר (_sappîr_), 'sapphire' evoking the cutting or polishing of a precious stone rather than its distinctive colour. The KJV's 'polishing' reflects this interpretation, which the Geneva Bible had already adopted in 1560. Cheyne, in _The Pulpit Commentary_ (1906, p. 65), considers the KJV 'substantially correct', though he prefers 'their form was (like) a sapphire'. For Cheyne, the comparison concerns not bodily outline but a gleaming, brilliant appearance. This reading fits the chapter's earlier precious-stone imagery, but its lexical basis remains uncertain. BDB gives 'cutting, polishing' (or 'beauty of form') as the sense here, but it rests on this single occurrence, since the other seven mean a separate area.

Salters lists more specific proposals that identify the feature as hair or facial hair: Hillers suggests 'their beards', Löhr emends the word to נִזְרָתָם (_nizratām_), connecting it with the reference to hair in Jeremiah 7:29.

A perhaps more natural alternative is to understand the sapphire as describing blue veins beneath the skin parallel to the red comparison before it. Blayney translates the phrase 'their veining was the sapphires' (Blayney, p. 403), interpreting the three comparisons as a progressive anatomical description: white skin, red flesh visible beneath it, and blue veins. The association of גזר (_g-z-r_) with dividing or intersecting provides the basis for this interpretation. It predates Blayney: Gill reports that Braunius took the word to mean the veins which 'variously intersect the flesh like sapphirine rivers' (in Gill, on Lam 4:7)[^4], and Adam Clarke (Clarke, p. 3) later restates the analogy in terms of the skin, flesh and veins of a fine complexion.

The veins interpretation is coherent, particularly if the comparisons describe the body's visible colouring, but it is no more securely established than the hair proposal. Both depend on inferring a bodily meaning from a root whose other biblical occurrences are architectural. The same standard of evidence should therefore apply to both.

Salters (p. 303) ultimately favours the hair interpretation, citing Song of Songs 7:5 and an Egyptian description of the god Re with hair of lapis lazuli:

> It is, perhaps, unlikely that anyone's hair in the ancient Near East was anything but black, but healthy lustrous black hair may/does reflect the blue sky (the healthier, the bluer) and gives the impression of being blue or purple

So the sapphire comparison is read as an allusion to the lustre of healthy black hair, rather than its literal colour. This is possible, but depends on comparative evidence rather than a clear Hebrew meaning for _gizratām_.
## On Salters' translation

Having set out Salters' reasoning, I turn to the points at which it depends on interpretative choices that the Hebrew does not compel.

The first question is whether נְזִירֶיהָ must be understood as 'her nobles.' Salters does not claim to have established an unambiguous meaning. He draws on the root sense of 'setting apart,' on the Joseph passages (Gen 49:26; Deut 33:16), and on Renkema's contextual argument that the ordinary term for princes (שָׂרִים) had already been used for those driven out, exiled or hanged. These considerations make 'nobles' a plausible reading; they do not make it the necessary one. The same root sense of separation applies equally, and more directly, to the Nazirites themselves -- the group whose very designation derives from the vow that sets them apart. Salters objects that the biblical passages referring to Nazirites speak only of abstinence, corpse-avoidance and uncut hair, never of beauty, yet the description in Lamentations 4:7 is not a catalogue of Nazirite regulations but a poetic recollection of an extraordinary appearance that has now been lost. Nothing in the text requires that appearance to belong exclusively to a social elite rather than to a group marked by religious consecration.

The traditional reading carries a burden of its own, which Salters is right to press. Numbers 6 never ties the Nazirite to a complexion, and nothing in the legislation explains why consecration should be accompanied by a body of snow, flushed and sapphire-marked. Three considerations lessen the difficulty without removing it. First, Amos 2:11-12 sets Nazirites beside the prophets as a gift of God to Israel and pairs them with young men (בַּחוּרִים), which shows that they were a visible and honoured institution, and therefore a plausible element of a poet's memory of Jerusalem's lost glory. Second, the Nazirite's status is expressed in the language of sacredness and adornment. The hair is called _nēzer ʾĕlōhāyw_, 'his consecration to God' (Num 6:7), from the same root as the diadem (_nēzer_) on the high priest's turban (Exod 29:6), and Numbers 6:5 declares him 'holy'. Sapphire belongs to the same register: it is set in the high priest's breastplate (Exod 28:18), forms the pavement beneath God's feet (Exod 24:10) and the likeness of his throne (Ezek 1:26). > The imagery of gems and radiance is therefore compatible with the sacred status of the consecrated, as well as with the social distinction of nobles. Whilst the burden remains, the description is no better explained by rank than by consecration.

Salters' preference for 'nobles' seems to place some weight on a further assumption, namely that the poem is deploying hyperbole to evoke an ideal of 'refined' beauty (p. 299), and that unusually pale skin was regarded as a mark of social distinction. His suggestion, quoted above (p. 301), is a possibility, but it is not demonstrated by the text, nor is it required by the comparisons with snow and milk, comparisons which establish brightness or purity of colour. Salters acknowledges that one of the verbs, זכה, can carry a moral sense, though he rejects it here; but neither the moral nor the physical sense, by itself, establishes an aesthetic hierarchy in which the palest is the most refined. Even if such a hierarchy existed, it would not settle the identity of the subjects. A complexion compatible with the social distinction of nobles is equally compatible with the religious distinction of Nazirites.

There is some biblical evidence for a social or aesthetic significance attached to complexion. In Song of Songs 1:5-6, the speaker describes herself as dark (שְׁחוֹרָה) yet lovely, and excuses her darkness by saying that the sun has looked upon her (שֶׁשְּׁזָפַתְנִי הַשָּׁמֶשׁ) as her brothers have made her work in the vineyards. Her explanation also suggests that exposure to the sun was a significant consideration, and that a complexion less affected by outdoor labour was valued.  This does not, however, establish that extreme whiteness was an ideal of beauty, [^5]or that the description in Lamentations is intended to express admiration. Nor can the relatively modest difference between sun-exposed and sheltered skin readily account for the extraordinary comparisons with snow and milk in Lamentations. A preference for an un-weathered complexion might help explain the positive significance of pallidity of complexion, but it does not fully explain the extremity of the description.

If this aesthetic framework is adopted, it must also apply to the final clause. Once the subjects are imagined as embodiments of refined beauty, the sapphire comparison is drawn toward the feature that can most readily be made to serve that ideal: lustrous hair that reflects sapphire-coloured light. Yet hair is also the one bodily marker the biblical texts repeatedly associate with the Nazirite, namely the uncut hair required by the vow. The hair reading therefore sits at least as comfortably with the traditional understanding of נְזִירֶיהָ as with the 'nobles' reading that Salters prefers. Notably, hair could also be prized in a prince (2 Sam 14:25-26), so it does not decide between them. Furthermore, the textual warrant for identifying גִּזְרָתָם with hair is itself slender, and in Salters' discussion rests heavily on comparative and iconographic material rather than on clear Hebrew usage. The same is true of the veins reading discussed above, and the same standard ought to be applied to both.

In short, Salters' translation is a coherent and carefully argued interpretation but not the only coherent interpretation the Hebrew will support.
## The immediate context: Lamentations 4:1-8
### Verses 1-5

Any case for 'her Nazirites' must reckon with the way the chapter frames its subjects before verse 7. The poem opens with a lament over debased preciousness: the gold has become dim, and the 'precious stones' (Salters) or 'stones of the sanctuary' (KJV) are scattered at the head of every street' (4:1, KJV) or 'public place' (4;1, Salters). Verse 2 applies the same language of value to persons: the 'precious sons of Zion' (KJV) or 'precious people of Zion' (Salters), comparable to 'fine gold', are now valued as little as earth pots (4:2). Verse 5 adds the language of former abundance: those who ate 'dainties' (Salters) are desolate in the streets, while those 'brought up in scarlet' (KJV, Salters) are now amongst 'dunghills' (4:5, KJV/Salters).

This context establishes a contrast between former preciousness and present degradation, but does not necessarily establish the subjects' social rank. 'Sons of Zion' identifies them with Jerusalem; the references to delicacies and scarlet suggest former abundance, but need not describe a separate aristocratic or 'noble' class. Nor does the chapter confine its concern to the privileged: it also speaks of mothers (4:3), infants and children (4:4). The comparison with fine gold expresses metaphorically the value attributed to the people, not necessarily their position in society or actual financial worth. A person need not be wealthy or noble to be precious.

The gem imagery of verse 7 is already prepared by verses 1-2, where gold and precious stones provide the language for describing the people. This makes the comparisons with *penînîm* and sapphire less surprising, but also raises the possibility that they express worth and radiance rather than literal anatomy. The alternatives need not be mutually exclusive: a physical description can simultaneously convey preciousness.

The streets/public places frame the chapter's reversals in verses 1, 5 and 8, keeping the public visibility of the fall in view. The context therefore supports a reading of verse 7 in terms of former distinction and present degradation, but does not determine the nature of that distinction.
### Verse 8: the inversion

Verse 8 answers verse 7 closely, though not term for term. Whiteness and brightness give way to a darkness stronger than soot (חָשַׁךְ מִשְּׁחוֹר, _ḥāšaḵ miššāḥôr_), and what was luminous becomes dry as wood. Verse 8 also supplies the only unambiguous bodily term in the passage, עוֹר (_ʿôr_) 'skin', and the colours of verse 7 are usually read back onto it.

That inference deserves scrutiny. Even in verse 8 the darkness is predicated not of skin but of תָּאֳרָם (_tōʾŏrām_), 'their appearance'. Skin appears only in the following clause, as what shrivels upon the bones. Verse 7 names no surface at all. The reading of its colours as skin colours is therefore an inference, and one the poem could readily have made explicit, as Job 30:30 does for עוֹרִי שָׁחַר (_ʿôrî šāḥar_) 'blackened skin.' Hebrew does not require a body part in such descriptions: the beloved is צַח וְאָדוֹם (_ṣaḥ wĕʾādôm_) in Song 5:10 with none named, and David is simply אַדְמֹונִ֔י (_ʾadmōnî_) (1 Sam 16:12). The absence of _ʿôr_ in verse 7 may therefore mean little. It may equally indicate that the colours describe the whole appearance, or even radiance, rather than a complexion, and that skin enters only when the surface is ruined.

A further point concerns the noun תֹּאַר (_tōʾar_), 'form' or 'bodily appearance', of which _tōʾŏrām_ is the suffixed form. It is often found in the expressions יְפַת תֹּאַר and יְפֵה תֹּאַר (_yĕpat tōʾar_ / _yĕpê tōʾar_), 'beautiful in form' (Gen. 29:17; 39:6; 1 Sam. 25:3; Esth. 2:7), but there the evaluation is supplied by the adjective, and verse 8 does not use this idiom. What the verse describes is a loss of colour and luminosity ('darker than soot'; יָבֵשׁ כָּעֵץ, _yābēš kāʿēṣ_, 'dry as wood'), which is as consistent with the physical effects of famine described in 4:9-10 as with the loss of an aesthetic ideal. To read the loss as one of beauty, and that beauty in turn as a mark of social refinement, adds two interpretative moves that the text does not warrant. Verse 8 therefore neither requires the beauty framework nor supports it over a reading in which the earlier appearance was simply striking or distinctive.

Recognisability is a further feature of the inversion, and it bears on the beauty framework. The poem says that the subjects are 'not recognised in the streets' (לֹא נִכְּרוּ בַּחוּצוֹת), and the cause given is the darkening of their appearance. Non-recognition after a change of condition is not unique to this passage (compare Job 2:12, where Job's friends, seeing him from afar, do not recognise him[^6]), but here the change is the complete inversion of a colour described in verse 7: from a brightness compared to snow and milk to a blackness exceeding soot. The poem presents the darkening of their appearance as what makes the subjects unrecognisable; it does not say that they have lost their rank or their beauty. This is compatible with the colours of verse 7 having been features by which they were known, though the extremity of the antithesis may also be rhetorical, and the passage does not show which. It does not require Salters' ideal of refined beauty, and the possibility that they were recognisable, set apart, נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_), because of an unusual appearance deserves consideration alongside it.

None of this establishes that the subjects were Nazirites. It shows that the features usually offered against the traditional reading, that Numbers 6 mentions no complexion and that the description is aesthetic, are not decisive, because the same description can be read as marking out a visibly distinctive group, and _nāzîr_ means 'set apart.' The word is not attested for a specifically visible difference, and I do not claim that it is. What remains is a lexical possibility, a contextual consistency (the chapter's gem and consecration language, Amos 2:11-12), and a modest observation about recognisability. The contextual evidence therefore leaves the traditional reading open, without establishing that the subjects were Nazirites.
## Three construals

The colours of verse 7 can be viewed in three ways, which differ in what they take the poem to be describing.

In the first, the poem describes an ideal complexion: fair, flushed and fine-veined, admired and associated with nobility. This is Salters' framework, and it has real support. Song 1:5-6 attests a premium on un-weathered skin, Song 5:10 describes the beloved as 'white and ruddy' (Song 5:10) from the same roots as _ṣāḥû_ and _ʾādmû_, and ruddiness is a mark of beauty in David (1 Sam 16:12; 17:42). The beauty associations of _tōʾar_ in verse 8 fit the same reading.

In the second, the poem draws on a register of radiance rather than complexion. Song 5:14 describes the beloved's body as ivory overlaid with sapphires, and the Vulgate's _ebore antiquo_ in 4:7 may reflect the same pairing. On this reading, sapphire and _penînîm_ are images of worth and luminosity, in keeping with the gold and holy stones of 4:1-2, and not descriptions of individual features. On this reading, sapphire need not identify a particular blue feature of the body: the comparison belongs to the chapter's wider language of preciousness and radiance.

In the third, the poem describes a body that is visibly unusual: one whose appearance marked out its bearers with a notable combination of brightly white skin flushed red, possibly with visible blue veins.

Anatomy alone cannot discriminate between them. Very fair skin may reveal the underlying blood vessels more visibly, with veins appearing blue beneath the skin, a common observation, so Blayney's progression from surface to flesh to vessels may indeed describe a fair complexion. The question is how an ancient audience would have heard the description, as ideal or as marked, and here the vocabulary is not unambiguous. Snow is the standard comparison for skin whitened by patches of צָרַעַת (_ṣāraʿat_) (Exod 4:6; Num 12:10; 2 Kgs 5:27), so 'white as snow' was not necessarily a neutral compliment. Ruddiness, while a mark of beauty in David, is also the sign of Esau's difference (Gen 25:25).

What may help is how the description functions in its setting. The poem frames the loss in verse 8 as a loss of recognition, and the cause it gives is the inversion of the colours. A general ideal of beauty does not by itself explain this, whereas a distinctive appearance could: the poem may be recalling features by which the subjects were recognised, not merely qualities by which they were admired. This leaves the third construal open, possibly in combination with either of the others, and it harmonises with the sense of being 'set apart' by visible distinction as well as by vow, or even rank.

The nearest comparison for the whole cluster lies outside the canon of the Hebrew Bible. 1 Enoch 106:2-3[^7]  describes the new-born Noah as 'white as snow and red as a rose', with hair like 'white wool' and eyes that 'light the house,' leading his father Lamech to fear that the child is not his, and to say that the baby's form is unlike that of men and is like that of the children of heaven (106:5). The Genesis Apocryphon has a parallel account. Both are far later than Lamentations, and cannot be used as evidence for how it was read in its day, but they do show that the same culture did have available these colour terms for a body understood as extraordinary - and not necessarily as beautiful. The luminous white hair of Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:14, and the body 'like beryl' of Daniel 10:6, suggest that this idiom either belonged to or developed into a wider register.

Of the three, the third construal gives the most direct account of the subjects' loss of recognisability, although the poem does not require it.
## Conclusion

 The traditional identification as 'her Nazirites' remains possible if not established; nor does the Hebrew clearly privilege Salters' hair interpretation of _gizratām_. His account is coherent, but its aesthetic framework is an interpretative choice rather than a requirement of the text. The poem's framing of the loss in verse 8 as a loss of recognition offers one reason to consider whether the colours of verse 7 were features by which the subjects were known, rather than simply admired. This is a modest claim that does not identify the subjects or fix the meaning of any single term. It suggests, however, that the question of what the subjects looked like should be considered alongside another: what function does their appearance serve in the poem?.
