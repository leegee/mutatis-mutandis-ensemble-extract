# Distinguished by Appearance: Reconsidering Salters' Lamentations 4:7–8

## Abstract

Lamentations 4:7 describes the former appearance of Jerusalem's _nəzîrêhā_ in terms of snow, milk, a red mineral and sapphire, and the sense of nearly every term is disputed. This essay examines how David Salters' 2010 ICC commentary resolves those disputes, rendering the subjects as 'nobles' and the description as an ideal of 'refined beauty.' It reconstructs his philological reasoning and the history of interpretation behind it, and argues that his translation is coherent but not required by the Hebrew. In particular, the traditional 'Nazirites' remains lexically viable, and the hair reading of _gizratām_ is no better supported than the alternatives. The essay then asks what verses 1-8 imply about the function of the description, and proposes that the colours are presented as that which makes the subjects publicly identifiable, a feature that an ideal of beauty alone does not explain. The argument is tentative and does not intend to offer a new translation, but to show that assumptions about aesthetic ideals have shaped the reading of a physical description whose terms remain open, and it outlines how a larger study could test the proposal.
## Introduction: an unresolved physical description in Lamentations 4:7

The earliest English renditions of Lamentations are generally associated with the Wycliffite Bible translations of the closing decades of the fourteenth century. The passage offers a striking description of an exceptional group:

> Whittere is Nazareis þan ſnoȝ, ſhynendere þan mylc; reddere þan old yuer, þan ſafyr fairere.

— Early Wycliffe

> Nazareis therof weren whitere than snow, schynyngere than mylk; rodier than elde yuer, fairere than safire.

— Late Wycliffe

> Her Nazirites were purer then snow, they were whiter then milke, they were more ruddie in body then rubies, their polishing was of Saphir.

— King James Version

> Her nobles had been brighter than snow, more dazzling than milk; in body they were ruddier than coral, their hair (shone) like sapphire.

— Salters (2010)

Considered individually, each version presents an unusual physical description. Considered together, their differences suggest a significant interpretative problem. These are not simply alternative choices of English vocabulary, but reflect different understandings of the underlying Hebrew, developed through distinct traditions of translation and interpretation. The disagreement is particularly striking because the passage appears to describe an exceptional physical appearance of some kind, yet neither its precise features nor the identity of those described has been established beyond dispute.

This essay examines the interpretative reasoning underlying the translation of this passage in David B. Salters' _Lamentations: A Critical and Exegetical Commentary_ (2010), published in the International Critical Commentary series, a major and widely respected work of modern biblical scholarship. His commentary provides an extensive philological and textual examination of the book, drawing on the Masoretic Text, ancient versions, earlier commentaries and modern scholarship. His treatment of 4:7–8 is particularly valuable for its detailed consideration of the highly debated Hebrew expressions and the history of their interpretation. His translation is thus the outcome of a closely reasoned interpretation, which he explains in considerable detail.

I shall draw heavily upon Salters' analysis of the Hebrew and the scholarship he cites, both to establish what remains uncertain and to identify the assumptions underlying his own interpretation. I shall set out his conclusions at the end of each section before considering the principal uncertainties surrounding the subjects' identity and the descriptions of whiteness, redness and sapphire, and then examining how the immediate context might inform their interpretation. I argue that Salters' rendering of the subjects as ‘nobles’, and his interpretation of their appearance as an ideal of ‘refined’ physical beauty, offer a coherent reading but are not required by the Hebrew. The traditional understanding of נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_) as ‘her Nazirites’ remains lexically viable, while the precise physical referents of the subsequent comparisons are less certain than any single translation might suggest. In particular, the contrast between the extraordinary appearance described in verse 7 and the darkening and deterioration of the subjects in verse 8 invites consideration of whether the passage describes a visually distinctive appearance rather than simply an ideal of beauty.

My purpose is not to establish a definitive alternative translation, but to examine what the Hebrew and its interpretative history leave open, and to consider whether assumptions about physical appearance have sometimes narrowed the range of possible readings.
## The history of interpretation

### Defining the subject: Jerusalem's Nazirites, or not?

The subject of the passage is indicated by נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_), traditionally translated 'her Nazirites' or 'her consecrated ones'. The word derives from נָזִיר (_nāzîr_), related to the root נזר (_n-z-r_), meaning to separate or set apart. Its familiar biblical use is for someone consecrated to God, particularly a person undertaking the Nazirite vow described in Numbers 6:1-21, which includes abstention from wine, avoidance of contact with the dead and the prohibition against cutting one's hair. This traditional interpretation is supported by the Septuagint, Peshitta, Vulgate, Targum and Symmachus, as well as Midrash Rabbah, Rashi, Calvin and a number of modern commentators. It is also reflected in the Wycliffe versions, the Douay-Rheims and the KJV.

Several alternatives have nevertheless been proposed. Some scholars consider the Masoretic reading to be the result of a scribal error and emend the word to נְעָרֶיהָ (_nəʿārêhā_), 'her young people' or 'her youths'. 

Rabbi Joseph Kara (c. 1050–1125) offers a more radical interpretation, understanding נְזִירֶיהָ as ‘her hair’, by analogy with נִזְרֵךְ (_nizrēḵ_) in Jeremiah 7:29. This makes the passage a description of hair becoming white through illness, rather than of a group of people. As Salters notes, this reading requires זַכּוּ (_zakkû_) to be understood as a declarative perfect, describing a present rather than a past condition. As such it sits less comfortably with the chapter's recurring contrast between former glory and present degradation.

A further interpretation, found in _Midrash Leqah Tob_, derives the sense 'princes' (שָׂרֶיהָ) from the idea of being set apart or distinguished, via Nahum 3:17. This reading is the reading of Luther and several other interpreters.

Salters draws on Albrektson and Renkema in developing a broader, non-technical understanding of _nāzîr_ as someone distinguished or pre-eminent. Albrektson cites Genesis 49:26 and Deuteronomy 33:16, where Joseph is described as the _nāzîr_ of his brothers, and Renkema offers a contextual argument, that the poem could not appropriately use the ordinary Hebrew term for 'princes' (_śārîm_), given the fate of Jerusalem's rulers already recounted elsewhere in the poem, so  proposes the less specific 'nobles'. Salters questions the dependence of this argument on the unity of the book, but nonetheless it is 'nobles' used in his translation.

Yet the interpretation of _nāzîr_ as 'noble' remains open to question. Neither Genesis 49:26 nor Deuteronomy 33:16 necessarily makes it a formal designation of political or aristocratic rank or privileged social rank - it may there equally describe Joseph as separated, set apart or distinguished from his brothers, or even as one consecrated among them. 

### White

There is little disagreement over the basic lexical meaning of זַכּוּ (_zakkû_), a form of זכה (_zākâ_), meaning 'to be clean, pure or bright'. The word can also carry a metaphorical sense of moral purity, as in Psalm 51:6 and Job 15:14 and 25:4, but Salters considers that unlikely here. The parallel verb צַחוּ (_ṣāḥû_), also associated with brightness or whiteness, and the subsequent references to redness and darkness suggest that both verbs describe physical appearance rather than moral qualities.

Salters follows Brenner in arguing that the comparisons with snow and milk give the verbs a specifically white sense, since both substances are used elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible as comparisons for whiteness. He also rejects the possibility that the description refers to clothing, arguing that 'the passage goes on to speak of the body' (p. 301). He therefore understands the comparisons as referring more specifically to the subjects' skin, while regarding the language as 'hyperbolic':

> the skin of someone living in Palestine is likely to be a shade of olive, but it is possible that the paler the skin the more refined a person was thought to be.

We shall return to this observation in due course.
### Red

Salters notes that the second half of verse 7 is difficult. The Masoretic Text reads אָדְמוּ עֶצֶם מִפְּנִינִים (ʾādmû ʿeṣem mippənînîm), approximately 'they were redder in body than penînîm'. Both עֶצֶם (ʿeṣem) and פְּנִינִים (penînîm) are uncertain. The former, ordinarily 'bone' and by extension 'body' or 'substance', is singular where the plural verb would lead one to expect a plural or collective subject. Salters follows the KJV and several modern scholars in treating it as an adverbial accusative, 'in body', which avoids the agreement problem, although he acknowledges Gottlieb's objection that this is forced. The ancient versions offer no clear resolution: the Septuagint omits the word, while the Vulgate has 'old ivory' and the Peshitta 'their bones'. The Targum and Rashi broaden it to 'appearance'.

The verb אָדְמוּ (_ʾādmû_), 'they were red', is less disputed. The difficulties lie in what is red, which is the question of _ʿeṣem_, and in the comparison. Elsewhere _penînîm_ occurs in Job 28:18 and Proverbs 3:15, 8:11, 20:15 and 31:10, always as a standard of value, never of colour. Its precise identity is uncertain, and coral, pearls and other precious stones have all been proposed and all have rebuttals. Salters considers that the verb constrains the interpretation to some extent: whatever _penînîm_ denotes, it must be compatible with redness. He accordingly reads the clause as describing a ruddy physical appearance, without claiming certainty about either the material or the function of עֶצֶם.
### Blue

The final expression, סַפִּיר גִּזְרָתָם (_sappîr gizratām_), presents a further difficulty. _Gizratām_ is rare, derived from גזר (_g-z-r_), 'to cut' or 'divide', and its only other biblical occurrences, in Ezekiel 41-42, refer to a separated room or forecourt, which does little to establish a bodily sense here. Salters notes that nothing indicates which physical feature is meant, and the versions reflect this. The Septuagint ('their pieces') stays closest to the root, Symmachus gives 'their limbs', and others generalise: 'their body' (Peshitta), 'their form' (RSV, Keil, Rudolph), 'their appearance' (Luther). The Targum is unusually specific ('their faces'), and Jerome's _pulchriores_, 'more fair', is, in Salters' words, a free rendering.

More specific proposals identify hair. Hillers suggests 'their beards', on the strength of Sumerian and Egyptian representations in which lapis lazuli is associated with beards, hair and eyebrows, though Salters notes that this identification is conjectural. Löhr emends _gizratām_ to נִזְרָתָם (_nizratām_), 'their head-hair', after Jeremiah 7:29. Salters strengthens the case with Song of Songs 7:5 and an Egyptian description of Re, whose hair is of genuine lapis lazuli, and suggests:

> It is, perhaps, unlikely that anyone's hair in the ancient Near East was anything but black, but healthy lustrous black hair may/does reflect the blue sky (the healthier, the bluer) and gives the impression of being blue or purple

#### Blue veins: Braunius, Blayney and Clarke

On page 303, Salters briefly mentions Benjamin Blayney's (1728-1801) interpretation, where  the final phrase is 'their veining was the sapphires'. His reading treats the three comparisons as elements of a single description, with a clear visual progression: from the overall whiteness of snow- and milk-white skin, to the redness of the flesh visible beneath it towards the bone, and finally to the blue veins intersecting its surface. He derives the veins comparison from the sense of גזר (_g-z-r_), ‘to divide’ or ‘intersect’, which he takes to describe the pattern of the veins beneath the skin.

This interpretation predates Blayney: John Gill attributes it to Johannes Braunius, while Adam Clarke subsequently restates the analogy:

> Milk will most certainly well apply to the whiteness of the skin; the beautiful ruby to the ruddiness of the flesh; and the sapphire, in its clear transcendent purple, to the veins in a fine complexion.

The reading is coherent, particularly if the comparisons are understood as describing the body's visible colouring. It is not, however, demonstrably stronger than the proposed reference to hair. גִּזְרָתָם (_gizratām_) is thinly attested, and the veins interpretation, like the hair interpretation, depends on inferring a bodily meaning from a root whose other biblical occurrences are architectural. The same standard of evidence should therefore be applied to both.

Although Salters' translation lands on 'hair (shone) like sapphire,' he concludes his discussion by acknowledging that the precise meaning of גִּזְרָתָם (_gizratām_) remains uncertain, and that there is no consensus over whether the comparison refers to form, appearance, limbs, veins, hair, beard or colour. 
## On Salters' translation

Salters' translation runs:

> Her nobles had been brighter than snow, more dazzling than milk; in body they were ruddier than coral, their hair (shone) like sapphire.

Having examined Salters' reasoning in detail, I turn now to the points at which he acknowledges uncertainty, and to the interpretative assumptions that may influence how those uncertainties are resolved.

The first question is whether נְזִירֶיהָ must be understood as 'her nobles.' Salters does not claim to have established an unambiguous meaning. He draws on the root sense of 'setting apart,' on the Joseph passages (Gen 49:26; Deut 33:16), and on Renkema's contextual argument that the ordinary term for princes (שָׂרִים) had already been used for those driven out, exiled or hanged. These considerations make 'nobles' a plausible reading; they do not make it the necessary one. The same root sense of separation applies equally, and more directly, to the Nazirites themselves -- the group whose very designation derives from the vow that sets them apart. Salters objects that the biblical passages referring to Nazirites speak only of abstinence, corpse-avoidance and uncut hair, never of beauty, yet the description in Lamentations 4:7 is not a catalogue of Nazirite regulations but a poetic recollection of an extraordinary appearance that has now been lost. Nothing in the text requires that appearance to belong exclusively to a social elite rather than to a group marked by religious consecration.

The traditional reading carries a burden of its own, which Salters is right to press. Numbers 6 never ties the Nazirite to a complexion, and nothing in the legislation explains why consecration should be accompanied by a body of snow, flushed and sapphire-marked. Three considerations lessen the difficulty without removing it. First, Amos 2:11-12 sets Nazirites beside the prophets as a gift of God to Israel and pairs them with young men (בַּחוּרִים), which shows that they were a visible and honoured institution, and therefore a plausible element of a poet's memory of Jerusalem's lost glory. Second, the Nazirite's status is expressed in the language of sacredness and adornment. The hair is called _nēzer ʾĕlōhāyw_, 'his consecration to God' (Num 6:7), from the same root as the diadem (_nēzer_) on the high priest's turban (Exod 29:6), and Numbers 6:5 declares him 'holy'. Sapphire belongs to the same register: it is set in the high priest's breastplate (Exod 28:18), forms the pavement beneath God's feet (Exod 24:10) and the likeness of his throne (Ezek 1:26). A description in terms of gems and radiance is therefore no more strange for the consecrated than for the noble. Third, Genesis 49:26 and Deuteronomy 33:16 apply _nāzîr_ to Joseph, who is elsewhere the Bible's most conspicuously beautiful man (Gen 39:6), though this is a coincidence of reference and not suggested as a semantic link between the root and beauty. Whilst the burden remains, the description is no better explained by rank than by consecration.

Salters' preference for 'nobles' seems to place some weight on a further assumption, namely that the poem is deploying hyperbole to evoke an ideal of 'refined' beauty, and that unusually pale skin was regarded as a mark of social distinction. He writes that 'the skin of someone living in Palestine is likely to be a shade of olive, but it is possible that the paler the skin the more refined a person was thought to be.' This is a possibility, but it is not demonstrated by the text, nor is it required by the comparisons with snow and milk, comparisons establish brightness or purity of colour. Salters acknowledges that one of the verbs, זכה, can carry a moral sense, though he rejects it here; but neither the moral nor the physical sense, by itself, establishes an aesthetic hierarchy in which the palest is the most refined. Even if such a hierarchy existed, it would not settle the identity of the subjects. A complexion compatible with the social distinction of nobles is equally compatible with the religious distinction of Nazirites.

There is some biblical evidence for a social or aesthetic significance attached to complexion. In Song of Songs 1:5-6, the speaker describes herself as dark (שְׁחוֹרָה) yet lovely, and excuses her darkness by saying that the sun has looked upon her (שֶׁשְּׁזָפַתְנִי הַשָּׁמֶשׁ) as her brothers have made her work in the vineyards. Her explanation also suggests that exposure to the sun was a significant consideration, and that a complexion less affected by outdoor labour was valued.  This does not, however, establish that extreme whiteness was an ideal of beauty, or that the description in Lamentations is intended to express admiration. Nor can the relatively modest difference between sun-exposed and sheltered skin readily account for the extraordinary comparisons with snow and milk in Lamentations. A preference for an un-weathered complexion might help explain the positive significance of pallidity of complexion, but it does not fully explain the extremity of the description.

If this aesthetic framework is adopted, it must also apply to the final clause. Once the subjects are imagined as embodiments of refined beauty, the sapphire comparison is drawn toward the feature that can most readily be made to serve that ideal: lustrous hair that reflects sapphire-coloured light. Yet hair is also the one bodily marker the biblical texts repeatedly associate with the Nazirite, namely the uncut hair required by the vow. The hair reading therefore sits at least as comfortably with the traditional understanding of נְזִירֶיהָ as with the 'nobles' reading that Salters prefers. Notably, hair could also be prized in a prince (2 Sam 14:25-26), so it does not decide between them. Furthermore, the textual warrant for identifying גִּזְרָתָם with hair is itself slender, and in Salters' discussion rests heavily on comparative and iconographic material rather than on clear Hebrew usage. The same is true of the veins reading discussed above, and the same standard ought to be applied to both.

So whilst the hair reading, which Salters is careful to note as conjectural, is possible, it is not the only reading the Hebrew will bear, and it only becomes more attractive once one has already decided that the verse is celebrating an idealised physical perfection.

In short, Salters' translation is a coherent and carefully argued interpretation. It is not, however, the only coherent interpretation the Hebrew will support. The traditional rendering 'her Nazirites' remains lexically viable. The assumption that the poem is idealising refined beauty, and that pale skin is its primary marker, is an interpretive overlay rather than a necessary inference from the text. Once that overlay is set aside, the uncertainties Salters himself records about the precise referent of עֶצֶם, the identity of פְּנִינִים, and the meaning of גִּזְרָתָם remain open. Alternative construals of the colours of Lamentations 4:7 continue to deserve consideration.

## The immediate context: Lamentations 4:1-8

### Verses 1-5

Any case for 'her Nazirites' must reckon with the way the chapter frames its subjects before verse 7. The poem opens with a lament over debased preciousness: the gold has become dim, and the 'precious stones' (Salters) or 'stones of the sanctuary' (KJV) are scattered at the head of every street/public place' (4:1, KJV/Salters). Verse 2 applies the same language of value to persons: the 'precious sons/people of Zion' (KJV/Salters), comparable to 'fine gold', are now valued as little as earth pots (4:2). Verse 5 adds the language of former abundance: those who ate 'dainties' (Salters) are desolate in the streets, while those 'brought up in scarlet' (KJV, Salters) are now amongst 'dunghills' (4:5, KJV/Salters).

This context establishes a contrast between former preciousness and present degradation, but does not necessarily establish the subjects' social rank. 'Sons of Zion' identifies them with Jerusalem; the references to delicacies and scarlet suggest former abundance, but need not describe a separate aristocratic or 'noble' class. Nor does the chapter confine its concern to the privileged: it also speaks of mothers (4:3), infants and children (4:4). The comparison with fine gold expresses metaphorically the value attributed to the people, not necessarily their position in society or actual financial worth. A person need not be wealthy or noble to be precious.

The gem imagery of verse 7 is already prepared by verses 1-2, where gold and precious stones provide the language for describing the people. This makes the comparisons with penînîm and sapphire less surprising, but also raises the possibility that they express worth and radiance rather than literal anatomy. The alternatives need not be mutually exclusive: a physical description can simultaneously convey preciousness.

The streets/public places frame the chapter's reversals in verses 1, 5 and 8, keeping the public visibility of the fall in view. The context therefore supports a reading of verse 7 in terms of former distinction and present degradation, but does not determine the nature of that distinction.

### Verse 8: the inversion

Verse 8 answers verse 7 closely, though not term for term. Whiteness and brightness give way to a darkness stronger than soot (_ḥāšaḵ miššāḥôr_), and what was luminous becomes dry as wood. Verse 8 also supplies the only unambiguous bodily term in the passage, skin (_ʿôr_), and the colours of verse 7 are usually read back onto it.

That inference deserves scrutiny. Even in verse 8 the darkness is predicated not of skin but of _tōʾŏrām_, 'their appearance'. Skin appears only in the following clause, as what shrivels upon the bones. Verse 7 names no surface at all. The reading of its colours as skin colours is therefore an inference, and one the poem could readily have made explicit, as Job 30:30 does for blackened skin (_ʿôrî šāḥar_). Hebrew does not require a body part in such descriptions: the beloved is _ṣaḥ wĕʾādôm_ in Song 5:10 with none named, and David is simply _ʾadmōnî_ (1 Sam 16:12). The absence of _ʿôr_ in verse 7 may therefore mean little. It may equally indicate that the colours describe the whole appearance, or even radiance, rather than a complexion, and that skin enters only when the surface is ruined. The root _šḥr_ also appears in Song 1:5-6, where the speaker is _šĕḥôrâ_, 'dark', through exposure to the sun. Whatever else the darkening of verse 8 conveys, it echoes a vocabulary already associated with weathering and a life lived outdoors, which would sit naturally with a reversal of social fortune.

The link between the verses that matters most concerns the final clause of verse 7. That verse ends with a feature marked by a third-person plural suffix, _gizratām_, and verse 8 opens with _tōʾŏrām_, 'their appearance', as the thing that has darkened. _Tōʾar_ denotes form or bodily appearance and is most often found in the idiom _yāpeh/yĕpat tōʾar_, 'beautiful in form' (Gen 29:17; 39:6; 1 Sam 25:3; Esth 2:7). Its associations of attractiveness favour the framework discussed above, and I acknowledge that they cut Salters' way. Since the colour of verse 8 attaches to _tōʾar_, however, it is at least possible that the sapphire element of verse 7 is likewise a feature of general appearance, as the RSV, Keil and Rudolph take it ('their form') and Luther ('their appearance'), rather than of hair alone. The pairing also makes sense of the structure: verse 7 would close on the look of the whole person, and verse 8 would then name that same look as what has darkened. This is an argument from structure, not a proof, but it stands in the way of the hair reading, since on Salters' own account hair in the ancient Near East was black already, and hair that is already black can hardly grow darker than soot.

Recognisability is the second thing the inversion offers, and it is my reply to the beauty framework. 'They are not recognised in the streets' (לֹא נִכְּרוּ בַּחוּצוֹת) suggests that the change described in verse 8 has removed something by which the subjects were previously distinguished. The relationship between the verses is significant here: verse 7 describes an extraordinary combination of colours that verse 8 then removes with a description of the darkened appearance. The colours in verse 7 identify a distinctive physical appearance, the loss of which makes the subjects unrecognisable. This does not necessitate conventional beauty. The possibility that they were recognisable, set apart, נְזִירֶיהָ (_nəzîrêhā_), because of an unusual appearance deserves consideration alongside the more familiar interpretation of their former appearance as an ideal of aristocratic beauty. This does not establish they were Nazirites but it does lend some weight to that point of view.
## Three construals

The colours of verse 7 can be viewed in three ways, which differ in what they take the poem to be describing.

In the first, the poem describes an ideal complexion: fair, flushed and fine-veined, admired and associated with nobility. This is Salters' framework, and it has real support. Song 1:5-6 attests a premium on unweathered skin, Song 5:10 describes the beloved as 'white and ruddy' (צַח וְאָדוֹם) from the same roots as _ṣāḥû_ and _ʾādmû_, and ruddiness is a mark of beauty in David (1 Sam 16:12; 17:42). The beauty associations of _tōʾar_ in verse 8 fit the same reading.

In the second, the poem draws on a register of radiance rather than complexion. Song 5:14 describes the beloved's body as ivory overlaid with sapphires, and the Vulgate's _ebore antiquo_ in 4:7 may reflect the same pairing. On this reading, sapphire and _penînîm_ are images of worth and luminosity, in keeping with the gold and holy stones of 4:1-2, and not descriptions of individual features. The question of which feature is blue would then be misplaced, and the colours would belong to the chapter's governing metaphor of value. The question of exactly what is blue would remain.

In the third, the poem describes a body that is visibly unusual: one whose appearance marked out its bearers, brightly white skin flushed red with remarkably visible deep blue veins.

Anatomy alone cannot discriminate between them. Very fair skin may reveal the underlying blood vessels more visibly, with veins appearing blue beneath the skin, a common observation, so Blayney's zoom from surface to flesh to vessels may indeed describe a fair complexion. The question is how an ancient audience would have heard the description, as ideal or as marked, and here the vocabulary is not unambiguous. Snow is the standard comparison for skin whitened by patches of  _ṣāraʿat_ (Exod 4:6; Num 12:10; 2 Kgs 5:27), so 'white as snow' was not necessarily a neutral compliment. Ruddiness, while a mark of beauty in David, is also the sign of Esau's difference (Gen 25:25).

What can discriminate is how the description functions in its setting. As argued above, the poem treats the subjects' appearance as something by which they were publicly known, and its loss as the loss of recognition. A shared ideal of beauty does not obviously do that, whereas a distinctive look does. This tells in favour of the third construal, possibly in combination with either of the others, and it harmonises with the sense of being 'set apart' by visible distinction as well as by vow or rank. I do not suggest that _nāzîr_ means 'visibly different'. The word is not attested in that sense, and the suggestion is only that the connotation of separation sits comfortably with it.

The nearest comparison for the whole cluster lies outside the canon of the Bible. 1 Enoch 106:2 describes the new born Noah as 'white as snow and red as a rose', with hair like 'white wool' and eyes that 'light the house,' leading his father Lamech to fear that the child is not his, and to say that the baby's form is unlike that of men and is like that of the children of heaven (106:5). The Genesis Apocryphon has a parallel account. Both are far later than Lamentations, and cannot be used as evidence for how it was read in it day, but they do show that the same culture did have available these colour terms for a body understood as extraordinary. The luminous white hair of Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:14, and the body 'like beryl' of Daniel 10:6, suggest that this idiom belonged to a wider register, and I would treat these only as the beginning of a thread.

The conclusion the evidence supports is a limited one. All three construals are coherent, none is required by the Hebrew, and the choice among them appears governed more by assumptions about beauty than by the text. The recognisability of the subjects is the one element in the passage that bears on the choice, and it points away from a general ideal and towards a distinctive appearance.
