### **Key Exegetical Points noted by Salters:**

1. **Context & Imagery**: Salters observes that because actual princes/rulers (_śārîm_) are described earlier in the book as having been exiled or killed (1:6, 2:9, 5:12), the poet used _nəzîreyhā_ ("nobles" / "distinguished ones") as a distinct synonym to convey the downfall of Jerusalem's elite[4].
2. **Rejection of Emendation**: Salters agrees with Bertil Albrektson in rejecting Ehrlich/Rudolph's emendation to "young men", pointing out that _nāzîr_ carries the sense of "prince" or "noble" in passages like Genesis 49:26 and Deuteronomy 33:16[4]. - He asserts that with less attention to detail than given to this use. Surely both those other passages can be read differently?

> 	The interpretation of _nəzîrêhā_ as “princes” or “nobles” is frequently supported by Genesis 49:26 and Deuteronomy 33:16, where Joseph is described as the _nāzîr_ of his brothers. Neither passage, however, necessarily requires a specifically political or aristocratic meaning. The expression can be understood as referring to Joseph as one separated, set apart or distinguished from his brothers, whether through his particular circumstances, his status within the family, or his pre-eminence. Moreover, Deuteronomy substantially repeats the expression in Genesis, so the two passages should not be treated as wholly independent lexical attestations. They establish the possibility of a broader, non-technical sense of _nāzîr_, but do not by themselves establish that “princes” or “nobles” is the appropriate interpretation in Lamentations 4:7.
