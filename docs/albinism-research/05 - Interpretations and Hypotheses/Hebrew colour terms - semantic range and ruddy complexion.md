# Hebrew colour terms — semantic range and ruddy complexion

## Question

Can וְאָדֹם (*wəʾādōm*) in Song of Songs 5:10, and the related redness language in Lamentations 4:7, reasonably be understood as a pink or rosy complexion? The user’s rationale is that an ancient colour term need not divide the visual spectrum exactly as modern English terms do.

## Findings

**Yes: “pinkish,” “rosy,” or “ruddy” complexion is a historically attested and contextually reasonable reading.** It is not a leap from “red” to “pink” when the referent is a person and the adjacent imagery describes brightness or whiteness. It is a contextual interpretation of an ancient colour category, rather than a claim that וְאָדֹם is a dedicated Hebrew equivalent of modern English *pink*.

The distinction matters: the lexical colour family is plainly red/ruddy; the shade perceived in a particular human description is reconstructed from syntax, co-text, poetic setting, and comparative usage. In the two passages at issue, a rosy cast against a pale or luminous complexion fits those cues well.

## Why modern colour labels do not map one-to-one

Biblical Hebrew has fewer ordinary colour terms than modern English, and colour terms can cover a broader range of hues and brightness values. “Red” may name the colour of blood or wine, but the same lexical family also appears with lentils, a red cow, and horses—objects whose visible shades are not all the same red. This supports a broad red/ruddy category; it does not mean every referent had an identical hue.

Justin Reed makes the complexion implication explicit: the אָדֹם family is used of David (1 Sam 16:12; 17:42), the beloved in Song 5:10, and the earlier Nazirite figures in Lam 4:7. Reed reports the possible complexion range in scholarship as **pinkish** (citing Brenner 1982, 72–74) through **brown or brownish-red** (citing Keel 1994, 198; Longman 2001, 170). Reed stresses that mapping the biblical term onto a modern shade is partly subjective because one Hebrew term can cover distinctions English expresses with several terms.

This is direct support for treating “pinkish” as a serious scholarly possibility, not merely a modern reader’s imaginative gloss. The disagreement over the precise shade remains real.

## The two passages

### Song of Songs 5:10

> דּוֹדִי צַח וְאָדוֹם דָּגוּל מֵרְבָבָה

The beloved is described with coordinated צַח (*ṣaḥ*, bright/clear/white) and וְאָדֹם (*wəʾādōm*, and red/ruddy). Rashi’s plain-sense comment makes a complexion reading explicit: the young man is white, with a ruddy face. That is strong reception evidence for a red or rosy facial tone alongside paleness. Rashi’s gloss does not determine a precise modern shade, but it confirms that the colour pair was read as a bodily complexion, not as two abstract blocks of uniform colour.

A natural contextual rendering is **“My beloved is bright/fair and ruddy”**; **“fair and rosy”** or **“clear-skinned and pink-cheeked”** brings out one plausible complexion image but adds specificity not stated morphologically in the verse.

### Lamentations 4:7

The Masoretic form אָדְמוּ (*ʾādmû*) is a Qal perfect, third-person plural verb: “they were/became red” or contextually “ruddy.” It is related to, but not grammatically the same form as, the adjective וְאָדֹם in Song. Lamentations’ preceding snow/milk brightness and the disputed comparison in the rest of the verse may make a pink or rosy cast an attractive reading. The morphology of אָדְמוּ itself, however, does not mean “pink,” “blushing,” or “pink-cheeked”; those are contextual interpretations.

## Comparative parallel: 1 Enoch 106

The infant Noah is described in Greek as “whiter than snow and redder than a rose” (λευκότερον χιόνος καὶ πυρρότερον ῥόδου), with the body as the referent; the pairing appears in 106:2 and 106:10. The incomplete Aramaic witness preserves a word for “red,” while the fuller Greek and Ethiopic traditions preserve the extended description. This is especially relevant to the proposal that red language applied to a human need not mean uniformly scarlet skin and may evoke a rosy cast alongside whiteness.

This is **comparative imagery, not Hebrew lexical proof**: πυρρός is Greek, and the Aramaic word is not the Hebrew form אָדֹם. It strengthens the contextual plausibility of rosy human colouring in ancient literature without establishing that Song, Lamentations, and Enoch share a fixed colour category or identical meaning. See [[1 Enoch 106 - Noah's birth]]; Kelley Coblentz Bautch, “Peter and the Patriarch: A Confluence of Traditions?,” pp. 24–26.
## Evidence and inference kept distinct

- **Lexical evidence:** אָדֹם and related forms belong to the red/ruddy colour family.
- **Usage evidence:** the family applies to varied referents, including people; it therefore does not identify a single fixed shade in every context.
- **Contextual evidence:** Song pairs it with צַח and a portrait of the beloved; Rashi reads the contrast as white complexion and ruddy face. Lamentations pairs human subjects with snow/milk comparisons.
- **Interpretive conclusion:** “rosy/pinkish complexion” is a defensible reading in these passages and has scholarly support. “Pink” is a modern shade label used to communicate that contextual impression, not a narrow lexical definition of אָדֹם.
- **Not established:** the exact hue, the specific bodily site in Lamentations, whether the redness indicates blush, health, sun exposure, or another cause, and whether both passages intend precisely the same complexion.

## Methodological notes

1. Begin with the referent and the phrase in context, not the root’s etymology alone. A root association cannot by itself decide the exact visual shade.
2. Compare the term across referents and periods, while recognizing that Biblical Hebrew, later Hebrew, and modern Hebrew are not interchangeable systems.
3. Keep hue distinct from brightness and saturation. צַח / צחו may describe brightness, clarity, or whiteness; translating every occurrence as the modern neutral category “white” can flatten that dimension.
4. Use “ruddy” as a useful English bridge term: it signals human redness without implying a wholly red body. “Rosy/pinkish” is a more specific but reasonable contextual proposal where the surrounding description supports it.
5. Do not infer a modern Hebrew colour boundary from the fact that contemporary Hebrew uses וָרֹד for pink and אָדֹם for red. That modern distinction cannot establish the exact boundaries of an earlier Hebrew semantic field.

## Working conclusion

For Song of Songs 5:10, **“fair/bright and rosy-ruddy”** is a sound interpretive reading. For Lamentations 4:7, **“they were ruddy/rosy”** is likewise plausible in the full colour sequence. “Pink” is not an unwarranted leap; it is an informed modern label for one plausible part of a broader ancient red/ruddy range. Preserve “red/ruddy” in the lexical gloss and identify “pink/rosy complexion” as the contextual reading.
