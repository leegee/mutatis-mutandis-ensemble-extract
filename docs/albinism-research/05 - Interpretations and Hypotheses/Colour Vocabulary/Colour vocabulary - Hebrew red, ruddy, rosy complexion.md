# Hebrew ruddy complexion — lexical range, object exemplars and symbolism

## Research question

What visual range did Biblical Hebrew communicate through the אדם (_ʾ-d-m_) colour family, particularly when describing human complexion? What can the associated material and natural exemplars tell us about possible red, reddish-brown, pinkish and rosy appearances?

More broadly, did combinations of exceptional whiteness or luminosity with redness represent a distinctive ideal of human appearance in ancient Jewish literature? What aesthetic, cultural or symbolic significance did this combination carry, and what was subsequently preserved, transformed or lost through Greek, Latin and English transmission?

The investigation distinguishes three related but separate questions:

1. **Lexical meaning:** What did the Hebrew colour terms mean, and what range of appearances could they encompass?
    
2. **Contextual appearance:** What physical appearance did particular descriptions plausibly communicate?
    
3. **Symbolic significance:** What associations did the combination of whiteness, luminosity and redness carry in its literary and cultural context?

## The אדם red/ruddy colour family

The principal Hebrew forms include:

|Form|Transliteration|Passage|Significance|
|---|---|---|---|
|אָדֹם|_ʾādōm_|Song 5:10|Red/ruddy adjective|
|אָדְמוּ|_ʾādmû_|Lam. 4:7|They were/became red or ruddy|
|אַדְמוֹנִי|_ʾadmōnî_|Gen. 25:25|Esau described as red/ruddy|
|אַדְמוֹנִי|_ʾadmōnî_|1 Sam. 16:12; 17:42|David described as ruddy|
|אֹדֶם|_ʾōdem_|Exod. 28:17; 39:10; Ezek. 28:13|Red precious stone, generally identified as carnelian/sardius|
|מְאָדָּם|_məʾoddām_|Nah. 2:4|Shields reddened|
|אֲדַמְדֶּמֶת|_ʾădamdemet_|Lev. 13|Reddish skin discolouration|

The lexical family is plainly associated with redness, but its application to different materials, animals, liquids and human beings demonstrates that it cannot be mapped onto a single modern shade of red.

The distinction between lexical meaning and contextual appearance is essential. The word may be glossed red/ruddy while the appearance it communicates in a particular human description is better understood as rosy, pinkish, reddish-brown or another intermediate shade.

The root association alone cannot determine the precise visual appearance.

## Why modern colour categories do not map directly

Biblical Hebrew has fewer ordinary colour terms than modern English. A single ancient term may encompass distinctions which contemporary English speakers would express through several different words.

Justin Reed (2020, p. 4) identifies this problem in discussing David, the beloved in Song of Songs and the Nazirites in Lamentations.

He notes that the אדם family is applied to all three groups and that their descriptions associate redness with attractive or healthy appearance.

Reed gives examples of the broader semantic range:

- Wine (Isa. 63:2).    
- Blood (2 Kgs. 3:22).    
- Precious stones, including the disputed _pĕnînîm_ in Lam. 4:7.    
- Cooked lentils (Gen. 25:30).    
- The red heifer (Num. 19:2).    
- Horses (Zech. 1:8; 6:2).    

These objects do not necessarily share an identical modern colour. The same lexical family may communicate appearances that English speakers would describe as red, orange, yellowish-brown, reddish-brown or other shades.

Reed reports a scholarly disagreement about the complexion of individuals described as _ʾādōm_ or related forms:

- **Pinkish:** Brenner (1982, pp. 72–74).    
- **Brown or brownish-red:** Keel (1994, p. 198); Longman (2001, p. 170, subject to verification of the precise Hebrew term under discussion).    

The precise shade remains contested.

This is not simply a problem of translation. It concerns the relationship between ancient colour categories, physical appearance and the way human complexion was described.

## Object exemplars and material associations

The material and natural referents associated with the אדם colour family offer a way of investigating the range of appearances communicated by the vocabulary.

It is important to distinguish explicit comparisons from ordinary lexical applications. Not every occurrence provides an object exemplar.

### Carnelian or sardius

**Exodus 28:17; 39:10; Ezekiel 28:13**

The gemstone אֹדֶם (_ʾōdem_) is generally identified as carnelian or sardius.

BDB identifies the stone through its association with redness. The Septuagint renders it _sardion_.

Carnelian occurs in descriptions of the priestly breastplate and in Ezekiel's description of precious stones associated with Eden.

Its visual range includes orange-red and reddish-orange appearances rather than one uniform saturated red.

This is a particularly interesting lexical relationship: the gemstone name and the red/ruddy colour family share the same root.

The ancient Near Eastern significance of red gemstones also deserves investigation. Noegel (2016) discusses red materials and their possible associations with blood, protection and fertility.

**Research questions:**

- What appearance did ancient observers associate with _ʾōdem_?
    
- How were carnelian and related red stones used in jewellery and ritual objects?
    
- Did their associations with blood, fertility or protection contribute to the symbolic significance of redness?
    

### Coral, pearls or rubies

**Lamentations 4:7**

The term פְּנִינִים (_pĕnînîm_) is disputed. Proposed identifications include:

- Coral.    
- Pearls.    
- Rubies or other precious stones.    

Hartley (2010, p. 110) recognises the uncertainty.

If coral is intended, Mediterranean precious coral (_Corallium rubrum_) offers a potentially important pink-to-red visual exemplar.

If pearls are intended, the comparison involves a very different material and appearance. If rubies are intended, the emphasis may be on a stronger red gemstone.

The identification matters to the interpretation of the Nazirites' complexion, but should not be resolved by assuming that one modern translation is definitive.

**Research questions:**

- What is the strongest historical and philological identification of _pĕnînîm_?
    
- Does the comparison concern colour, lustre, material value or some combination?
    
- Can coral support a pinkish or rosy interpretation without assuming that the Hebrew word itself means pink?
    

### Lentils

**Genesis 25:30**

הָאָדֹם הָאָדֹם (_hāʾādōm hāʾādōm_)

Esau asks for some of the red stuff, the red stuff.

The referent is cooked lentil stew rather than a standardised red pigment.

Depending on the preparation, lentils and their cooking liquid may produce reddish-brown, orange or earthy appearances.

This demonstrates the breadth of the colour family, although it does not establish the appearance intended in descriptions of human complexion.

### The red heifer

**Numbers 19:2**

פָרָה אֲדֻמָּה (_pārāh ʾădummāh_)

The prescribed animal is a red heifer.

This is a useful comparison because the colour describes an entire animal rather than a small object or a particular part of the body.

The precise acceptable shade is not specified by the adjective alone.

### Horses

**Zechariah 1:8; 6:2**

Red horses are described using the אדם colour vocabulary.

Horse coat colours provide a useful comparison, particularly reddish-brown and chestnut appearances.

The passages should be examined in their own literary and visual contexts rather than automatically assigned modern horse-colour classifications.

### Blood and wine

**2 Kings 3:22; Proverbs 23:31; Isaiah 63:2**

Blood and wine provide particularly familiar red exemplars.

In 2 Kings 3:22, the water appears red like blood. In Proverbs 23:31, wine is described through the verb יַאְדִּים (_yaʾdîm_), from the same colour family. Isaiah 63:2 uses red clothing and wine imagery.

These are useful contrasts with the possible lighter or more delicate complexion implied by human applications of the vocabulary.

### Summary of exemplars

|Passage|Hebrew expression|Referent|Evidential function|
|---|---|---|---|
|Gen. 25:30|הָאָדֹם|Lentil stew|Object associated with red|
|Num. 19:2|אֲדֻמָּה|Red heifer|Whole-animal colour|
|Exod. 28:17|אֹדֶם|Carnelian/sardius|Gemstone colour|
|2 Kings 3:22|אָדֹם|Blood-like water|Strong red comparison|
|Prov. 23:31|יַאְדִּים|Wine|Liquid colour|
|Isa. 63:2|אָדֹם|Red garments and winepress|Material and liquid association|
|Zech. 1:8; 6:2|Red colour terminology|Horses|Animal coat colour|
|Lam. 4:7|אָדְמוּ ... מִפְּנִינִים|Human appearance compared with precious material|Explicit complexion comparison|

These examples establish a broad semantic and material field. They do not establish that all the referents had the same colour or that every occurrence evokes the same symbolism.

## Human complexion: the principal passages

### Esau in Genesis 25:25

Esau is described as אַדְמוֹנִי (_ʾadmōnî_) at birth.

The description is associated with his redness and his covering of hair.

This is important evidence for the application of the colour family to human physical appearance.

Noegel (2016) argues that the description also participates in the narrative's association between Esau, Edom and Seir, and anticipates aspects of Esau's masculinity and physical prowess.

It should not automatically be interpreted as a description of red hair.

### David in 1 Samuel 16:12; 17:42

David is described as אַדְמוֹנִי (_ʾadmōnî_), ruddy.

The term appears alongside other favourable physical descriptions:

- יְפֵה עֵינַיִם (_yĕp̄ēh ʿênayim_): beautiful or attractive eyes.
    
- טוֹב רֹאִי (_ṭôḇ roʾî_): good-looking.
    

David's redness is therefore part of a favourable physical portrait.

However, its precise significance is disputed.

Clines (1995, pp. 221–223) argues that ruddy colouring signals physical beauty. Noegel (2016) challenges this interpretation, noting that the texts distinguish _ʾadmōnî_ from descriptions of handsomeness.

Noegel suggests that the redness instead signifies virility, fertility and masculine prowess.

This disagreement is important: the term may communicate something beyond general attractiveness.

### Song of Songs 5:10

> דּוֹדִי צַח וְאָדֹם דָּגוּל מֵרְבָבָה

The beloved is described using two coordinated terms:

- צַח (_ṣaḥ_): bright, clear, luminous or white.
    
- וְאָדֹם (_wəʾādōm_): and red/ruddy.
    

Several English translations render _ṣaḥ_ as white, but the term has associations with brightness, clarity and luminosity. Goldenberg (2003, p. 93), as cited by Reed, is relevant to this distinction.

The verse should not be reduced to two simple blocks of colour. It may describe a luminous or fair complexion combined with contrasting redness.

Rashi's interpretation is particularly important reception evidence: he understands the beloved as white with a ruddy face.

A reasonable contextual rendering is:

**"My beloved is bright/fair and ruddy."**

"Fair and rosy" or "clear-skinned and pink-cheeked" communicates a possible appearance more specifically, but adds detail not explicitly expressed by the Hebrew morphology.

Noegel (2016) offers a further interpretation, connecting the beloved's redness with virility and fertility. He also associates it with the scarlet lips of the female beloved in Song 4:3.

The question of whether the redness signifies beauty, health, vitality or sexual potency remains open.

### Lamentations 4:7–8

The Masoretic form אָדְמוּ (_ʾādmû_) is a Qal perfect, third-person plural verb: "they were/became red" or contextually "ruddy."

It is related to, but grammatically distinct from, the adjective וְאָדֹם in Song 5:10.

The description of the Nazirites includes comparisons with snow, milk, precious materials and sapphire.

The preceding descriptions establish exceptional brightness and lustre. The redness occurs within this wider chromatic and material sequence.

The precise interpretation of _pĕnînîm_ remains uncertain, but the passage may describe a combination of luminous whiteness and healthy reddish colouring.

The transition in verse 8 is particularly important: the former appearance of beauty and lustre is replaced by darkness, shrivelling and emaciation.

This is not simply a change of colour. It is a transformation from health and vitality to sickness and deprivation.

## The possible pink or rosy interpretation

The distinction between lexical meaning and contextual appearance must be maintained.

| Interpretation                                             | Evidential status                                |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| The אדם family means red/ruddy                             | Established lexical **gloss**                    |
| Its range is broader than modern English red               | Strongly supported by varied usage               |
| It can communicate pinkish or rosy human complexion        | Scholarly interpretation with contextual support |
| It can communicate brown or brownish-red complexion        | Alternative scholarly interpretation             |
| It specifically means blushing or pink-cheeked             | Not established lexically                        |

**Brenner (1982, pp. 72–74) supports a pinkish complexion interpretation.**

Keel (1994, p. 198) and Longman (2001, p. 170) are cited by Reed as supporting darker, brown or brownish-red interpretations. The precise lexical item each author discusses should be checked against the original publications, particularly in Longman's case, because _ṣaḥ_ and _ʾādōm_ must not be conflated.

The important point is that pink is not an arbitrary modern invention. It is a plausible contextual interpretation within the broader ancient red/ruddy category.

However, it should remain a contextual interpretation, not a narrow lexical definition.

### Why rosy colouring is plausible

In Song 5:10, redness is coordinated with brightness or luminosity.

In Lamentations 4:7, redness occurs in a description already characterised by exceptional whiteness and lustre.

Both passages concern human appearance, and both have favourable descriptions of the individuals before the contrast with their later condition or context.

A rosy cast against a pale or luminous complexion is therefore a reasonable reconstruction, although the exact shade and physical cause cannot be established.

## Related Hebrew beauty imagery

The Song of Songs contains other colour comparisons that should be considered alongside the אדם family, while keeping them lexically distinct.
### Scarlet lips and pomegranate

**Song of Songs 4:3; 6:7**

The woman's lips are compared to scarlet thread, while her temple or cheek is compared to a slice of pomegranate.

The pomegranate provides an independent natural exemplar for facial appearance. Its exposed flesh may range from pinkish-red to deeper red.

These images do not use the אדם root, but they demonstrate how Hebrew poetry communicates beauty and complexion through familiar coloured materials and objects.

### Song 5:10

The male beloved's brightness and "redness" may be read alongside the woman's scarlet lips.

Noegel (2016) interprets the male redness as a counterpart to the female scarlet imagery, proposing a relationship between sanguine colouring, fertility and sexual vitality.

This is an interpretative argument rather than an established lexical meaning.

### Lamentations 4:7–8

The Nazirites' initial appearance is characterised by brightness, "redness" and precious materials. The subsequent description reverses this visual condition.

The passage is particularly valuable because it connects colour, lustre, physical health and the loss of vitality.

## Noegel: redness, virility and fertility

Robert Noegel's 2016 article, "Scarlet and Harlots: Seeing Red in the Hebrew Bible," is important because it develops a symbolic interpretation that goes beyond the lexical question.

Noegel engages with David J. A. Clines' argument that ruddy colouring signifies physical beauty.

Clines observes that _ʾadmōnî_ appears alongside descriptions of attractive eyes and good looks in the accounts of David.

Noegel objects that the texts distinguish redness from handsomeness. Since beauty can be described without _ʾadmōnî_, he argues that the term must communicate something additional.

He also rejects the older suggestion, associated with August Klostermann, that David's ruddy appearance refers to hair colour.

In discussing Esau, David and the beloved in Song 5:10, Noegel proposes that redness signifies masculine virility, physical prowess and fertility.

He connects the male sanguine colouring with female fertility imagery, particularly blood and the scarlet lips of the beloved.

This gives the redness a possible sexual and reproductive significance rather than simply an aesthetic one.

### Questions raised by Noegel

- Does _ʾadmōnî_ communicate a quality beyond physical beauty?
    
- Is the redness associated with vitality, virility or fertility?
    
- Does the interpretation apply equally to Esau, David and the beloved in Song?
    
- Is the association culturally specific to particular narratives or more widespread in ancient Near Eastern symbolism?
    
- Can this interpretation be reconciled with Brenner's account of youthful good looks?
    
Noegel's interpretation should be retained as a significant competing hypothesis rather than adopted as the definitive meaning of the term.

## Achromaticity and the loss of colour in Lamentations 4

Søren Lorenzen (2024), "Precarious Hues: On Chromophobia, Chromophilia, and Transitions between Chromatic and Achromatic Colours in the Hebrew Bible," offers a particularly useful interpretation of Lamentations 4:7–8.

His discussion is relevant because it connects the loss of colour with the loss of health, lustre and vitality.

### Whiteness as luminosity

Lorenzen draws attention to the snow and milk comparisons in verse 7.

The descriptions may involve more than a neutral white hue:

- Snow can suggest glistening ice crystals as well as whiteness.    
- The relevant Hebrew vocabulary can convey brightness and lustre.    
- The comparison with milk may similarly evoke a luminous surface.    

The distinction between chromatic colour and brightness is important.

The original appearance may be better understood as radiant or glistening rather than simply white.

### Redness as youthful vitality

Lorenzen draws on Hartley (2010, p. 110) concerning the uncertain identification of _pĕnînîm_, and on Brenner (1982, p. 79) concerning the association of redness with youthful good looks.

He interprets the red colouring as part of a healthy, youthful complexion.

He also explores the association between bodily moisture, healthy bones and fertility, contrasting this with the dry, emaciated appearance of the Nazirites in verse 8.

The transition is therefore not merely from colour to absence of colour. It involves a loss of lustre, moisture and bodily vitality.

### Sapphire and lapis lazuli

Although the Hebrew term סַפִּיר (_sappîr_) is traditionally translated sapphire, the ancient stone is more likely to have been lapis lazuli in many biblical contexts.

Lapis lazuli has a deep blue appearance, often with golden pyrite inclusions and white calcite veins.

Lorenzen discusses the significance of this material and its possible associations with royal and divine imagery.

### 8.4 The transition in verses 7–8

The earlier appearance is characterised by:

- Snow-like brightness.    
- Milk-like whiteness and lustre.    
- Redness and healthy complexion.    
- Precious-material imagery.    
- Sapphire or lapis lazuli associations.    

The subsequent appearance is characterised by:

- Darkness.    
- Shrivelling.    
- Dryness.    
- Emaciation.    
- Loss of the former visual splendour.    

This is a particularly valuable example of a transition from positive chromatic and luminous imagery towards an involuntary achromatic state.

It may be useful for the wider thesis because it demonstrates that whiteness and colourlessness do not necessarily carry positive meanings independently of context. The loss of colour can signify suffering and the destruction of health.

## Comparative Jewish evidence: 1 Enoch 106

The birth of Noah in 1 Enoch 106 provides a particularly important comparative example.

The infant is described in the Greek tradition as:

> λευκότερον χιόνος καὶ πυρρότερον ῥόδου

"Whiter than snow and redder than a rose."

The description occurs in 106:2 and is repeated in the extended account.

The body is the referent, and the comparison combines exceptional whiteness with redness represented by a rose.

This is particularly relevant to the possibility that ancient descriptions of human redness could evoke a rosy appearance rather than uniformly saturated red skin.

However, the evidence must be kept distinct:

- The surviving Greek wording uses _pyrrhos_ and _rhodon_.    
- The surviving Aramaic evidence is incomplete and should be examined independently.    
- The Greek text cannot be used as direct proof of the meaning of Hebrew אָדֹם.    
- The comparison nevertheless demonstrates that white/red combinations could describe extraordinary human appearance in ancient Jewish literary traditions.    

See [[1 Enoch 106 - Noah's birth]] and Kelley Coblentz Bautch, "Peter and the Patriarch: A Confluence of Traditions?", pp. 24–26.

## Implications for English transmission

The significance of the Hebrew white/red and white/ruddy combinations extends beyond establishing the possible shade of an individual colour term.

The wider research asks whether these combinations encode a distinctive conception of human appearance: exceptional whiteness or luminosity accompanied by contrasting rosy or reddish colouring.

The English historical survey should investigate what happens to this relationship as the texts are translated and received.

Potential English expressions include:

- White and red.    
- White and ruddy.    
- Fair and rosy.    
- Pale and ruddy.    
- Snow and rose.    
- Rosy cheeks and pale complexion.    
- Bright or luminous skin combined with redness.
    
The research must distinguish three possible processes:

1. **Inherited symbolism:** English writers preserve imagery transmitted through Jewish and Christian scripture, translation and commentary.
    
2. **Transformation:** The original colour relationship survives but changes in vocabulary, intensity or symbolic meaning.
    
3. **Independent development:** English literary culture develops its own associations around pale skin, rosy cheeks, white hair and extraordinary appearance.    

Descriptions of pale complexions accompanied by rosy or red cheeks are particularly interesting as possible continuities.

Such continuities must be established through dated textual evidence rather than assumed from visual similarity.

The distinction between inherited Jewish symbolism and independently developed English conventions must remain explicit.

## Evidence and inference kept distinct

The following distinctions should govern all subsequent interpretation.

|Category|Current position|
|---|---|
|Lexical evidence|אדם and related forms belong to the red/ruddy colour family|
|Usage evidence|The family applies to varied materials, animals, liquids and people|
|Contextual evidence|Song 5:10 combines brightness/luminosity and redness|
|Contextual evidence|Lam. 4:7 combines human redness with snow/milk and precious-material imagery|
|Reception evidence|Rashi interprets Song 5:10 as white complexion with a ruddy face|
|Scholarly interpretation|Brenner supports a pinkish complexion possibility|
|Competing interpretation|Keel and Longman are cited for brown or brownish-red possibilities; exact terms require verification|
|Symbolic interpretation|Noegel proposes virility, fertility and masculine prowess|
|Literary interpretation|Lorenzen identifies the loss of colour and lustre as part of the destruction of health and vitality|
|Comparative evidence|1 Enoch 106 combines snow-like whiteness with rose-like redness|

### Not established

- The exact modern colour equivalent of _ʾādōm_ in any individual passage.    
- That the Hebrew word itself means modern English pink.    
- That _pĕnînîm_ definitively means coral.    
- That the redness necessarily indicates blushing or pink cheeks.    
- That Song 5:10 and Lamentations 4:7 describe precisely the same complexion.    
- That all instances of _ʾadmōnî_ signify beauty, virility or fertility in the same way.    
- That the ancient Jewish examples share one fixed symbolic system.
    
## Methodological principles

1. Begin with the referent and the phrase in context, not the root's etymology alone.
    
2. Distinguish lexical meaning from contextual physical appearance.
    
3. Examine the syntax and grammatical form of each occurrence.
    
4. Compare the term across different referents without assuming that every referent has the same shade.
    
5. Distinguish hue from brightness, luminosity and saturation.
    
6. Treat object exemplars as material and visual evidence, while acknowledging uncertainty in historical identification.
    
7. Distinguish explicit comparisons from ordinary applications of the colour vocabulary.
    
8. Use "red/ruddy" as the lexical gloss. Use "pinkish/rosy" as a more specific contextual interpretation where the evidence supports it.
    
9. Do not infer ancient colour boundaries from modern Hebrew, where וָרֹד denotes pink and אָדֹם denotes red.
    
10. Keep inherited Jewish imagery separate from independent English literary conventions.
    
11. Record competing scholarly interpretations rather than resolving them prematurely.
