Various Romans writing on the Gauls and Germans describe fierce/angry eyes, and sometimes combined with the term blue.

Looking through pale eyes on a sunny day often results in a squint and/or lowering of the brow to cut out sunlight, which can look fierce.

In **poetry** (Virgil, Statius, Ovid) the eye-as-light metonymy (_lumina_, _φάεα_) is the norm; in **ethnographic prose** (Caesar, Tacitus, Ammianus, Sidonius) the eye is described in terms of **colour** (_caerulei_, _glaucis_) and **ferocity** (_truces_, _acies_, _φοβεροί_, _θηριωδεῖς_) — the "gaze" of the Northern other is a **weapon**, not a **lamp**.

**Caesar's _acies oculorum_** is the earliest and most striking: _acies_ is a military term ("sharpness of a spear-point"), and applying it to the _eyes_ makes the gaze literally a weapon. This is the same semantic field as _truces_ (fierce, brutal) in Tacitus - the eye as a **threat**, not a source of light.
    
**Ammianus' _θηριωδεῖδι_** (15.10.18) is "beast-like" - the Gauls' eyes are _animal_ in their ferocity. This is a different register from the "flaming eyes" of the _Aeneid_ or _Revelation_, which associate the eye with **fire/light** rather than with **predation**.
    
**Sidonius** is the only one who gets close to the _lumina_ register - _glaucis genis_ and _algoso concolor profundo_ are genuinely poetic, and the comparison to the sea's colour is a kind of luminous quality. But even here the word is _genis_ (cheeks/face), not _lumina_.

# Latin
## *Lumina Flamma*

> Literally "eyes with fire." This phrase is famously used in Virgil's _Aeneid_ (_stant lumina flamma_) to describe eyes that stare fiercely, often with anger or awe.

-- [Lewis & Short](https://lsj.gr/wiki/flamma):

### Virgil 

> _Portitor has horrendus aquas et flumina servat terribili squalore Charon, cui plurima mento canities inculta iacet; **stant lumina flamma**, sordidus ex umeris nodo dependet amictus._

-- Virgil **Aeneid 6.298–300** (Charon, the ferryman of the dead):

"His eyes stand fixed, blazing with flame" — the _stant lumina flamma_ line. _Flamma_ is an instrumental ablative: "with flame."


> _ad caelum tendens **ardentia lumina** frustra, — lumina, nam teneras arcebant vincula palmas._

-- Virgil **Aeneid 2.404–405** (Cassandra, dragged from the temple):

"Burning eyes" she strains toward heaven in vain — the _ardentia lumina_ line.

# Greek

**Revelation's Greek as a whole** is the most irregular in the NT: Semitic interference, case errors, Hebraisms, Aramaisms.  Dionysius of Alexandria flagged it in the 3rd century.

>David Aune takes about 50 pages in his commentary (WBC, Vol. 1) to delineate the many unusual syntactic features in the book of Revelation. He remarks: "The Greek of Revelation is the most peculiar Greek in the NT, in part because it exhibits interference from Semitic languages, perhaps both Hebrew and Aramaic. The peculiarity of the language of Revelation encouraged many copyists to make 'improvements' in the text, with the result that the problem of reconstructing the original text has been made very difficult. In some instances it appears that only a single MS (Alexandrinus or Sinaiticus) has preserved the original reading (e.g., Rev 13:10)....The Greek of Revelation is not only difficult and awkward, but it also contains many lexical and syntactical features that no native speaker of Greek would have written. Dionysius of Alexandria (died ca. AD 264) observes of the author of Revelation (Eusebius _Hist eccl_. 7.26) "... that his use of the Greek language is inaccurate, and he employs barbarous idioms [ἰδιώμασίν τε βαρβαρικοῖς], producing solecisms [σολοικίζοντα]" (pp. clxii, cxcix).

**But the "eyes like a flame of fire" formula is not archaizing at all.** It is a stock apocalyptic image inherited directly from the Hebrew prophetic tradition (Dan 10:6, Ezek 1:4, 1:27). 

The underlying simile ***eyes like fire*** is as old as Homer (_Il._ 1.104, 13.474). It is a conventional prophetic/apocalyptic formula, not a deliberate archaism.

| | Latin (*Aeneid*) | Greek (*Revelation*) |
|---|---|---|
| Eye-word | *lumina* — a **poetic metonymy** (archaizing, elevated) | *ὀφθαλμοί* — the **ordinary, everyday** word for "eye" |
| Fire element | Fused into the noun phrase (*flamma*, *ardentia*) | Added as a **simile** (*ὡς φλὸξ πυρός*) |
| Register | High poetic | Prophetic/apocalyptic formula |

Revelation's Greek here is **more prosaic and conventional** than Virgil's Latin. The "old-fashioned" quality comes not from the Greek itself but from the **source tradition** (Daniel, Ezekiel) — the author is quoting a 600-year-old prophetic formula in perfectly ordinary Koine syntax. The oddness of Revelation's Greek shows up elsewhere (the case errors, the Hebraic word order, the untranslated _ἀμήν_), not in this image.
