# Middle English whiteness and human appearance

## Scope and method

This note begins a Middle English survey of words and images for white, pale, fair, ruddy, and light-coloured hair or skin. It keeps human appearance evidence separate from white clothing, sacred light, moral symbolism, and non-human bodies. A Middle English use cannot by itself demonstrate continuity from Old English or a direct response to Hebrew; transmission needs to be established text by text.

The first online resources to use are the University of Michigan’s [Middle English Compendium (MEC)](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/mec/), which provides the [Middle English Dictionary (MED)](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/middle-english-dictionary/) and a searchable [Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/). The MEC is open access and covers 1100–1500, but its corpus is explicitly a large searchable collection rather than an exhaustive linguistic corpus. The MED’s quotations and sense divisions will be important for distinguishing complexion, brightness, hair colour, and metaphorical uses.

I searched the Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse directly through its public full-text interface. Counts below are interface result counts, not unique textual occurrences: the corpus contains multiple editions and manuscript witnesses, and some results are editorial matter, glosses, or non-human / non-complexion uses. ## Initial candidate: *Floris and Blauncheflour*

In the Middle English romance *Floris and Blauncheflour*, the king refers to “Blauncheflour with þe white syde” and calls her fair. A nearby description gives a horse one side “white so mylke” and the other red. The juxtaposition makes the passage a promising example for this project, but the phrase *white syde* is textually and semantically uncertain: the edition’s note reports interpretations ranging from a light aspect/complexion to long or flowing hair. It should not yet be cited as an unambiguous skin-colour description.

- [Middle English Texts Series edition, *Floris and Blauncheflour*](https://metseditions.org/read/DMv5B92Rtz1G6skxLH7K3RiMygGbpdwX) — searchable text, manuscript/edition apparatus, and editorial notes.
- [Open-access edition excerpt and discussion](https://www.sidestone.nl/openaccess/9789088903397.pdf), pp. 116–17; note 159 specifically flags the ambiguity of *white syde*.

This is a useful early lead, not yet a settled lexical datum. Next check the manuscript witnesses and the MED entries for *white*, *side*, and *fair*, then compare other romance descriptions before interpreting the phrase.

## Chaucer: Absolon in *The Miller’s Tale*

Chaucer gives a compact, securely locatable cluster of appearance terms for the parish clerk Absolon (*Canterbury Tales*, *Miller’s Tale* 3314–24): his hair shines like gold; his *rode* is *reed*; his eyes are grey; and his white surplice is compared to a blossom on a branch. The passage places a red facial *rode* beside golden hair and a conspicuously white garment. It is a valuable Middle English contrast for the project, though the garment’s whiteness must not be misreported as skin colour. Harvard’s text glosses *rode* here as “complexion” and *reed* as “ruddy.”

- [Harvard Geoffrey Chaucer Website, *The Miller’s Prologue and Tale*](https://chaucer.fas.harvard.edu/pages/millers-prologue-and-tale), lines 3314–24.
- [University of Toronto, *The Miller’s Prologue and Tale* from the Hengwrt manuscript](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/millers-prologue-and-tale-hengwrt-manuscript-canterbury-tales), useful for comparison with a manuscript-based witness.

This is evidence for red facial colouring within a literary portrait, not yet evidence for a red-and-white complexion ideal. Check the MED entry and manuscript variants for *rode* before drawing semantic conclusions.

## Search plan

Search the MEC corpus and verify promising results in their edited texts. Begin with spelling variants and collocations for *whit/white*, *fair*, *pale*, *rede/red/ruddy*, and hair terms; then search separately for “white as snow/milk/lily” and body-part descriptions. Record for each hit the work, line or page, manuscript witness, edition, date range, referent, literal/metaphorical status, and whether the phrase is an independent description or biblical translation/allusion.

### Direct MEC corpus search (15 November 2026)

Searches used the corpus full-text interface and its wildcard convention (`work*` finds forms such as *worker* and *working*). I checked **whit***, **whyt***, and **whyte*** separately; wildcarding *whit-* does not find the distinct *whyt-* / *whyte-* spellings. The interface supports proximity searches, which helped locate combinations while retaining orthographic variants.

- Exact phrase **“white as snow”** returned 7 matches in 6 items. The result list includes *The Romans of Partenay*, *Prosalegenden*, Lovelich’s *Merlin*, *History of the Holy Grail*, *Mandeville’s Travels*, and the 1850 *Holy Bible*. These are leads requiring context checks; the result snippet in *Partenay* includes square-bracketed editorial English (“her upper part white as snow”), so that wording must not be quoted as the Middle English text.
- **whit*** near **snow*** (within 40 characters) returned 49 matches in 22 items. The broad count includes duplicates and contextual false positives.
- **whyt*** near **snow*** returned 24 matches in 17 items; **whyte*** near **snow*** returned 14 matches in 11 items. In *St. Brandan: a medieval legend of the sea*, the prose Life of St Brandan says that on Sunday “all we be made as whyte as ony snow” to praise the Lord. This is a collective devotional / moral image, not a literal description of complexion. [St. Brandan item](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/CME00019) (the precise division and hit are available through the corpus result list).
- **whit*** near **face*** returned 29 matches in 23 items. A strong human-appearance candidate occurs in *Libeaus Desconus*: the hit reads “Melk whit was her face,” at line 944 in the indexed poem text (the hit’s apparatus refers to witness M and variant readings). This is an explicit face/complexion comparison, unlike white clothing or a spiritual metaphor. Verify the line against the item’s full text and manuscript apparatus before using it as a stable quotation. [Libeaus Desconus, item record](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/CME00144).
- **whit*** near **mil*** returned 29 matches in 19 items. Many hits concern white textiles (for example “milk white” sheets in the Auchinleck miscellany), entries in a dictionary, or other non-human referents. *Mil- / milk* wildcarding finds *milk* spellings but does not by itself catch every possible vowel variant such as *mylk*; run those separately when a milk-comparison search is central.

These searches show why raw counts cannot be treated as counts of pale people: repeated Chaucer witnesses, spiritual comparisons, textiles, editorial glosses, and broad proximity windows all inflate the totals. Record each candidate’s exact item, textual division, line/page, edition, witness, and referent before interpreting it. The MEC corpus is selective, so findings should be described as corpus examples rather than a complete Middle English canon. Expand coverage with text-specific editions and other searchable collections as needed. Distinguish the date of composition from the date of the surviving witness.

## Questions for the next pass

- Does *white syde* elsewhere mean complexion, bodily side, clothing, or hair?
- How do MED senses and quotations divide *white*, *fair*, and *pale* when applied to faces, skin, and hair?
- Are red/white bodily contrasts used for complexion, blush, blood, health, or beauty?
- Which Middle English biblical translations render Lamentations 4:7, Song of Songs 5:10, and related passages, and how do their choices compare with Old English and Latin traditions?
- Which occurrences describe unusually pale or white-haired people, and do the texts connect those traits to age, sanctity, illness, ancestry, or radiance?
