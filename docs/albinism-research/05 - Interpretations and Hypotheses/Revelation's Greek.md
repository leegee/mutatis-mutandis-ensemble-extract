# Thus have I heard:

David Aune (WBC) lists four possibilities without committing: (1) Hebrew original, (2) Aramaic original, (3) bilingual Greek composition with Semitic interference, (4) LXX imitation. The **bilingual-interference + LXX-imitation hybrid** is probably the most defensible synthesis: the author was a Hellenistic Jew with strong Hebrew/Aramaic competence who was _also_ deliberately shaping his Greek to echo the prophetic tradition, and it is often impossible to tell which of the two is responsible for any given irregularity.

The key methodological problem (flagged by Moț and by the Second Language Acquisition studies) is that many constructions labelled "Hebraisms" are either attested elsewhere in Greek, or are so common in the LXX that they could be imitation of the LXX rather than evidence of a Semitic first language. The line between "the author's Hebrew is showing through" and "the author is quoting Ezekiel" is often very thin.

# Semitic language transfer (the traditional majority view)

> While he writes in Greek, he thinks in Hebrew

-- R.H. Charles,  _Apocalypse of St. John_, 1920, p. cxliii

- **Hebrew _Vorlage_ translated into Greek** — R.B.Y. Scott (1928, _The Original Language of the Apocalypse_)
    
- **Aramaic _Vorlage_ translated into Greek** — C.C. Torrey, who argued the verb tenses point to Aramaic rather than Hebrew
    
- **Bilingual interference** (no separate source text) — Mussies (1971, _The Morphology of Koine Greek: As Used in the Apocalypse of St. John: A Study in Bilingualism_); the author composes in Greek but his Hebrew/Aramaic syntax shapes the output. This is the most widely held version.

# Imitation of OT (Ezekiel etc)

A newer and increasingly influential argument: the "irregularities" are not the author's own language failure but **deliberate _imitatio_** of the difficult style of his prophetic source texts. Ezekiel's LXX itself is full of the same kind of syntactic awkwardness (the vision passages especially), and John is systematically imitating it.  [PDF thesis](https://place.asburyseminary.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2546&context=ecommonsatsdissertations). This dissertation on this point argues that once you recognize the _imitatio_ pattern, many of the supposed "Semitic transfers" dissolve - they are not the author's native language leaking through, but a conscious literary choice to replicate the prophetic register.

# Greek idiolect (no Semitic transfer at all)

Laurenţiu Moț (2016, _Morphological and Syntactical Irregularities in the Book of Revelation_) argues that the Semitic-influence explanation is **overstated and partly driven by theological presuppositions**. He classifies 232 proposed solecisms and finds only ~45 are "actual" irregularities, and even those can be explained as inner-Greek variation (dialectal, register-based, or simply authorial idiolect) without invoking Hebrew or Aramaic at all.

# Rhetorical / visionary explanation

A minority view holds that some irregularities are **deliberate rhetorical devices** (climax, parataxis for prophetic cadence) or even a byproduct of the **ecstatic visionary experience** the text describes. This is rarely argued seriously on its own but sometimes combined with the others.
