# גִּזְרָתָם — form, division, and the “veins” proposal

## The Masoretic form

The last colon of Lamentations 4:7 reads:

> סַפִּיר גִּזְרָתָם  
> *sappîr gizrātām*

**גִּזְרָתָם (*gizrātām*)** is the feminine singular noun **גִּזְרָה (*gizrāh*)** with a third-person masculine plural suffix, “their.” The noun’s final ה becomes ת before the suffix. In context, the phrase is commonly construed as “sapphire \[was] their form,” with the copula and sometimes “like” supplied in translation. The Masoretic text has גזרתם, from גזר; it does not have נזרתם, a different form sometimes proposed by emendation.

The noun’s precise sense in this verse is uncertain. “Form,” “shape,” “stature,” “outline,” and “polishing/beauty of form” are interpretations of the phrase, not equally secure dictionary glosses.

## Concordance evidence

The noun גִּזְרָה is rare. BDB’s concordance gives it in these contexts:

| Passage | Form/context | Lexical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lamentations 4:7 | גִּזְרָתָם, “their ___” | Hapax in this sense/context. BDB suggests “cutting,” extended to “polishing” or “beauty of form,” as of a sapphire. The precise referent is disputed. |
| Ezekiel 41:12–15; 42:1, 10, 13 | הַגִּזְרָה, “the separate area” | The noun denotes a defined/separated section or area in Ezekiel’s temple vision. Ezekiel provides the noun’s main contextual evidence, but that architectural sense does not by itself settle its metaphorical use in Lamentations. |

The related verb **גָּזַר (*gāzar*)** is used in Biblical Hebrew for cutting or dividing: for example, Solomon orders the living child divided in 1 Kings 3:25; the Red Sea is divided in Psalm 136:13; and Elisha’s companion cuts wood in 2 Kings 6:4. In the Niphal, forms can mean “be cut off,” as in Lamentations 3:54 and Isaiah 53:8, or “be decreed,” as in Esther 2:1. See the linked BDB/concordance entries for the fuller form-by-form index.

BDB compares the verb with cognate forms in Arabic, Ethiopic, Aramaic, and later Hebrew, with the central idea “cut, divide.” This is a lexical comparison across related languages, not proof that every derived noun means a physical cut.

## How “veins” was proposed

The veins reading starts from the root’s “cut/divide” semantic field and imagines the branching or intersecting lines of veins visible beneath skin. If סַפִּיר evokes a blue stone, the final phrase could then complete a colour image: pale skin compared with milk, ruddy flesh or flush compared with coral, and blue veins like sapphire. That is a coherent visual proposal, especially for very pale skin through which blue-looking veins show.

Johann Braunius proposed veins in *De Vestitu Sacerdotum Hebraeorum* (1680). Benjamin Blayney’s translation and note (1784) made the proposal explicit as “their veining was the sapphires”; Adam Clarke later explained the sequence as white skin, ruddy flesh, and sapphire-coloured veins. The proposal is not Rashi’s interpretation: Rashi understands the phrase as their forms being like sapphire.

The key limitation is that **גִּזְרָה does not elsewhere mean “vein.”** The veins interpretation extends the root’s “divide/intersect” idea to a bodily image; it is not a direct concordance gloss. The Hebrew contains no separate word for veins, and its syntax does not label which body part the sapphire image describes.

## Competing readings and textual caution

- **Form or shape:** the traditional and lexically cautious approach, since גִּזְרָה relates to cutting/shaping and the phrase compares it with a gemstone.
- **Polishing or beauty of form:** BDB’s proposal, supported by the gemstone setting; “polishing” is still an interpretive extension.
- **Veins:** a colour-based proposal that combines “divide/intersect” with sapphire’s blue appearance. It fits one possible reading of the colour sequence but is not a lexical sense established elsewhere.
- **Hair, beard, or eyebrows:** proposals that attach the blue stone to a particular dark body feature. Some scholars have suggested emending the Masoretic גזרתם to נזרתם, associating it with hair; that changes the consonantal text and must be kept separate from interpretation of the MT as written.

The ancient versions also show that the phrase was difficult: translations have been understood as “their body,” “their faces,” “their pieces/division,” or a freer description of beauty. Their divergence confirms the uncertainty; it does not independently establish veins.
