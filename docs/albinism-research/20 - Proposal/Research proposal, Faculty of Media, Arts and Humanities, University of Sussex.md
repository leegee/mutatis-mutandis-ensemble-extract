## Provisional Title

Representing Visible Difference: Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Eyes in English Writing, c. 700-1650, with Comparative Evidence to 1914

## Introduction

This project investigates how English writers from the Old English period to c. 1650 described and interpreted extraordinarily pale or radiant human and human-like bodies, focusing on their reception in the biblical descriptions of the Ancient of Days and the Son of Man. It asks how unusually pale hair and skin, bodily radiance and notable eyes acquired meaning through scripture, Latin exegesis, vernacular translation and English literary writing. A bounded comparison of material from c. 1650 to 1914, set against Thomas White's research on the construction of the medicalised category of "albinism", will test whether earlier representations were transmitted, transformed, retained or displaced. The project combines philological, exegetical and literary-historical close reading with evaluated semantic retrieval across historical corpora, using computational methods to find scant evidence across religious and secular writing.

## Research contribution

### Research questions and significance

_How were the biblical and exegetical representations of extraordinary paleness and radiance received, adapted and interpreted in English writing from c. 700 to 1650, and what continuities, transformations and discontinuities can be traced in comparative material from c. 1650 to 1914?_

1. How were Lamentations 4:7, Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14 understood in their original contexts, and through Latin exegesis and vernacular reception? Which features and associations were retained, altered or omitted?
2. How did English writers describe such hair, skin and eyes, and how did age, status, genre, religious context and observers' reactions shape interpretation?
3. Where do marvellous, ethnographic or naturalising accounts resemble the biblical and exegetical repertoire, and where can a channel of reception be demonstrated?
4. In c. 1650-1914, were elements of the earlier repertoire transmitted, adapted, secularised, retained residually, combined with other traditions or displaced?
5. For a sparse and lexically unstable phenomenon, what can diachronic semantic retrieval recover that conventional lexical searching misses, and what does it systematically overlook or misrepresent?

The questions matter because descriptions of extraordinary paleness have rarely been studied together as a problem of literary representation, biblical reception and visible bodily difference. Understanding what such bodies meant before they were medicalised clarifies the material on which later discourses of race, pathology and identity drew, and may offer people with albinism and their parents a fuller account of how such bodies were once understood.

### Original contribution

White's thesis, _"Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours": A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650-1914_ (2011), shows how the conception of albinism developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity. This project investigates the earlier textual record and tests whether later representations relate to older repertoires.

My hypothesis is that the red/white imagery of Lamentations 4:7 and the representations of hair and radiance in Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14, together with Latin exegesis and vernacular reception, gave English writers a distinctive repertoire for representing unusual appearance. I test this without presupposing it: similar descriptions may reflect parallel development or independent observation, and discontinuity and unresolved relationships remain possible findings. The project contributes by reconstructing the English reception of this little-studied repertoire, distinguishing demonstrable transmission from resemblance, and evaluating semantic retrieval for a phenomenon whose vocabulary changes across periods and genres. It also aims to deliver a usable, provenance-preserving search interface for historians, responding to concerns about their limited engagement with digital methods (Hitchcock 2013).

### Critical engagement with existing research

The project builds on White (2011) for the later history of albinism; on Bede (2001) and on Copeland (1991) and Poleg (2013) for medieval biblical reception and translation; on Biggam (2012) and Németh (2021) for historical colour terminology; and on Alaric Hall (2007) for unusual bodies in Anglo-Saxon England. Auerbach (1959), Frye (1982) and Fabiny (2009) inform my treatment of figural and typological interpretation, and Daston and Park (1998) provide a framework for wonder and the marvellous. Raymond Williams's dominant, residual and emergent cultural forms (1977) will inform how I analyse persistence and change, and his _Keywords_ (1976) how words accumulate and shift meanings. Koselleck's distinction between word and concept (2004) frames how a concept of visible difference can precede, and be reshaped by, the medicalised term "albinism". Work on race and racialisation (e.g. Heng 2018) will inform the study where appearance becomes a mark of social distinction; the theoretical framework will be developed in response to the material.

Lamentations 4:7 is the starting point. It is the earliest instance of the red/white descriptive cluster whose English reception I will trace. Jerusalem's _nəzîrêhā_ are whiter than snow, brighter than milk and redder than _penînîm_, with a sapphire comparison. Vocabulary and syntax are disputed, and translations vary: the Vulgate's _rubicundiores ebore antiquo_, which the Wycliffite versions follow; the KJV has "rubies." R. B. Salters (2010) reads the passage as an idealised description of dark-haired nobles, but the Hebrew requires neither nobles nor hair. My close reading of Salters, supplied as a writing sample, argues that the passage also allows interpretation as a description of visibly distinctive appearance. Without proposing a new translation, it shows how an assumed ideal of beauty can narrow the meanings of a contested description. This informs an annotation scheme in which "ideal", "radiant" and "marked" are overlapping interpretations rather than fixed categories.

Medieval English readers encountered biblical material through Latin and vernacular versions, so source languages are not treated as equivalent channels of influence. English apocalyptic writing, including the _English Apocalypse_ (Campbell 2024), and illustrated Apocalypse manuscripts such as the Abingdon, Trinity and Bohun Apocalypses (Burrows 2017; Short 2016) provide direct textual and visual evidence of reception.

The foundational interdisciplinary principle of Sussex makes it a natural home for a project joining medieval English literary studies, historical linguistics, concept-led research and digital humanities. Dr Katie Walter's work on Middle English literature, particularly bodies, skin and the senses (ed. Walter 2013), is ideally placed to support the literary-historical core. Dr Justyna Robinson's expertise in historical semantic change and concept-led approaches, including her work on Linguistic DNA (Fitzmaurice et al. 2017) and with the Concept Analytics Lab, bears directly on the computational method of tracing representations of bodily difference later classified as albinism. ## Research methods

### Sources and scope

The core object is a description of a human or human-like body (including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures) in which paleness of hair or skin, radiance or notable eyes appear in whole or part, with attention to who observed, when and how. Conventional fair complexion is excluded where independent of these features, but attenuations of the white/red pairing into fair hair and a rosy or ruddy complexion are retained.

A dedicated chapter examines Lamentations 4:7, Daniel 7:9, Revelation 1:13-14 and relevant Latin exegesis, with bounded comparison to Song of Songs 5:10, Isaiah 1:18 and 1 Enoch 106:2-3. Work in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek is limited to descriptive and colour vocabulary, providing a philological baseline rather than assumed routes of transmission. The Vulgate, Bede and Jerome will be read with specialist advice and Year 1 Latin training (to be confirmed with the department).

The English investigation uses digital corpora and databases, with no specialist equipment, in four stages:

- **Old English, c. 700-1150:** lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, then close reading of starting points including _Wonders of the East_, Grendel's eyes and _ælfscȳne_.
- **Middle English, c. 1150-1450:** targeted searches and close reading of biblical, exegetical, encyclopaedic and historical writing, notably Bartholomaeus Anglicus's _De proprietatibus rerum_ (XV.7, "De Albania"), Trevisa's translation and Isidore's _Albani_, using the Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse and Middle English biblical translations.
- **c. 1450-1650:** EEBO-TCP as the principal computational and interpretive corpus across religious, literary and natural-historical genres.
- **c. 1650-1914:** a bounded comparison, initially using ECCO and a nineteenth-century collection chosen during the pilot, testing later accounts against White's findings.

### Annotation

"Sacral", "marvellous" and "naturalising" are analytical annotations, not successive stages or exclusive categories. A naturalising account invokes natural processes or physical causes; it need not be medically correct or describe disease. Separately, descriptions are annotated as potentially ideal, radiant or marking a visibly distinctive body. I also record age, features, genre, status, reaction, quotation or allusion, and uncertainty; borderline cases remain open.

### Semantic retrieval and evaluation

This component builds on an MSc in Knowledge-based Systems, over 25 years of professional software development and 12 months of developing a provenance-preserving semantic-search prototype. Forward searches from early descriptions and imagery, and backward searches from later terminology associated with albinism, will be investigated separately before their overlap is assessed.

Prototype code and case study: [https://github.com/leegee/mutatis-mutandis-ensemble-extract](https://github.com/leegee/mutatis-mutandis-ensemble-extract)

The encoder under evaluation is MacBERTh, pretrained on historical English c. 1450-1950 (Manjavacas and Fonteyn 2021). Its performance will be compared with BM25, normalised and lemmatised pattern searches, and hand-built historical lexicons. The prototype uses multiple contextual windows and embedding representations to test whether different scales recover different evidence. Hybrid retrieval and contrastive fine-tuning will be considered only if the pilot justifies them. Given the model's training range, Old and Middle English will rest mainly on lexical searching and close reading.

Evaluation uses a hand-annotated gold set drawn from textual anchors, passages identified by White and independent close reading. Precision and recall will be assessed by period and, where possible, genre and feature. I will identify passages found only by semantic or hybrid retrieval and ask whether they change interpretation or merely add examples. Quotations and biblical echoes will be distinguished from independent use, and false positives, corpus bias and training/search overlap documented. A prototype query based on a 1972 clinical description of an "albino" child in Malta retrieved the Son of Man description in the _English Apocalypse_. This is a preliminary lead, not proof of performance, and will be tested against the gold set.

### Ethical considerations

No human participants, interviews or personal data are involved. Ethical issues include historical racialised or offensive terminology, the representation of people with albinism, and the limits of inference from surviving texts. Historical terms will be contextualised. My personal experience of albinism informs the questions but does not determine interpretation; evidence, scholarly inference and personal perspective will remain distinct. Copyright and corpus licences will govern public release of texts or annotations.
