# Representing Visible Difference: Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Unusual Eyes in English Writing, c. 700-1650, with Comparative Evidence to 1914

## Introduction

This project investigates how English writers from the Old English period to c. 1650 described and interpreted extraordinarily pale or radiant human and human-like bodies, focusing on the reception of a biblical repertoire associated with the Ancient of Days and the Son of Man. It asks how white hair, radiance and unusual eyes acquired meaning through scripture, Latin exegesis, vernacular translation and English literary writing. A bounded comparison of material from c. 1650 to 1914 will test whether elements of these earlier representations were transmitted, transformed, retained or displaced in later accounts. It does this in the context of Thomas White's work on the construction of the medicalised image of "albinism." The project combines close philological and exegetical reading with evaluated semantic retrieval across historical corpora. Computational methods support the discovery of sparse evidence for historical and literary interpretation.

## Research contribution

### Questions and significance

*How was the biblical and exegetical representation of extraordinary paleness and radiance received, adapted and interpreted in English writing from c. 700 to 1650, and what continuities, transformations or discontinuities can be traced in comparative material from c. 1650 to 1914?*

The investigation is organised around five related questions:

1. How were the descriptions in Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14 interpreted in their source-language, culture and context, and through later Latin exegetical and vernacular contexts, and which features or associations were retained, altered or omitted in English reception?
2. How did English writers describe unusual hair, skin, eyes and bodily radiance, and how did age, status, genre, religious context and observers’ reactions affect their interpretation?
3. Where do marvellous, ethnographic or naturalising accounts resemble the sacral repertoire, and where can a channel of reception be demonstrated?
4. In later writing, particularly c. 1650-1914, were elements of the earlier repertoire transmitted, adapted, secularised, retained residually, combined with other traditions or displaced?
5. For a sparse and lexically unstable phenomenon, how much historically relevant evidence can diachronic semantic search through variable-windowed ensemble embeddings recover that conventional lexical searching misses, and what does it systematically overlook or misrepresent?

Historical descriptions of extraordinary paleness have rarely been studied together as a problem of literary representation, biblical reception and visible bodily difference. Thomas White’s thesis *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650-1914* (2011) shows that the conception of "albinism" developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity. This project investigates the earlier record and tests whether later representations relate to older textual repertoires; it does not attempt a second comprehensive history of albinism.

The principal hypothesis is that the Bible (Lamentations 4:7, Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14) together with Latin exegetical and vernacular exegesis and reception supplied English writers with a distinctive repertoire for representing extraordinary paleness and radiance. The project tests that influence without pre-assuming it: similar descriptions may instead reflect convention, parallel development or independent observation; unresolved relationships and discontinuity remain possible findings.

The project contributes by reconstructing the English reception of a sacral repertoire of extraordinary paleness, distinguishing demonstrable transmission from resemblance and parallel development; and evaluating diachronic semantic retrieval for an under-represented[^2]  phenomenon whose vocabulary changes across periods and genres. Retrieval results will be tested against lexical baselines and close reading.

### Research context

The project builds on White for the later history of albinism; Bede and scholarship on medieval biblical reception and translation (Copeland, Poleg, Minnis) for scriptural transmission; Biggam, Németh and Viti for historical colour terminology; and Alaric Hall for unusual bodies in Anglo-Saxon England. Auerbach, Frye and Fabiny inform the treatment of figural and typological reading. Williams, Ricoeur and Koselleck help distinguish cultural persistence and metaphorical convention from changes in vocabulary and concept. Daston and Park, Bynum and Greenblatt provide a framework for wonder and the marvellous. Critical race scholarship (Heng, Whitaker, Hall) will contextualise descriptions entangled with later racial thought without equating medieval categories with modern race.

A worked example is Lamentations 4:7. The verse describes Jerusalem’s *nəzîrêhā* as whiter than snow and brighter than milk, redder than *penînîm*, with a final comparison involving sapphire. Its terms and syntax are disputed: the Vulgate renders  *rubicundiores ebore antiquo* ("ruddier than old ivory"), a choice followed by the Wycliffite versions; the King James Version instead has "rubies" and "their polishing was of Saphir". R. B. Salters reads the verse as an idealised description of the "refined beauty" of nobles, but neither the identification of the subjects as nobles and the interpretation of *gizratām* as hair are not required by the Hebrew. The researcher’s close reading of Salters’ commentary argues that the passage equally describes a visibly distinctive body. Without proposing a new translation it demonstrates how an assumed ideal (in this case, of beauty) can narrow the possible meanings of a contested physical description. This informs an annotation scheme that records "ideal", "radiant" and "marked" as potentially overlapping readings rather than treating one as the default. 

Along with Lamentations, Daniel 7:9, Revelation 1:13-14 are examined in their historical contexts[^1] to establish a source repertoire whose conceptualisations are then sought in later transmission into English

Source languages are not treated as equivalent channels of influence: medieval English readers commonly encountered biblical material through Latin and vernacular versions. English apocalyptic writing, including the English *Apocalyptica*, provides direct evidence of reception. Selected illustrated Apocalypse manuscripts (including the Trinity and Bohun Apocalypses) are used for visual context rather than as a systematic iconographic survey.

The project connects with Sussex through its combination of medieval English literary history and digital humanities. Dr Katie Walter’s work on Middle English literature, bodies, skin and the senses is directly relevant to the literary-historical core. Dr Justyna Robinson’s work on historical semantic change and concept-led approaches, including the Concept Analytics Lab, is directly relevant to the computational method. Dr Walter and Dr Robinson have agreed to co-supervise, subject to formal university approval and funding. The Centre for Early Modern and Medieval Studies and The Keep provide a relevant research environment and archival resource. The precise training provision for Latin will be confirmed with the department. A 25-year career as a consultant software developer provide domain and project management experience. 24-months engaging with semantic search provide practical Digital Humanities experience.

## Research methods

### Sources, scope and interpretation

The principal investigation covers English writing c. 700-1650. The core object is a description of a human or human-like body (including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures) in which very pale hair accompanies another marked feature, or very pale hair or skin is itself presented as peculiar. Luminosity, unusual eyes, observers’ reactions and white clothing are recorded separately. Generic symbolism of white unattached to a body and snow or wool similes applied to non-bodies are excluded. Conventional fair complexion is excluded where independent of the features studied, but potentially relevant attenuations of a white-red pairing into fair hair and rosy or ruddy complexion are retained.

A dedicated chapter examines Daniel 7:9, Revelation 1:13-14 and Latin exegesis, with bounded comparison to Lamentations 4:7, Song of Songs 5:10, Isaiah 1:18 and 1 Enoch 106:2-3. Source-language work is limited to relevant colour vocabulary: Hebrew and Aramaic provide a philological baseline, not an assumed route of transmission. The Vulgate and selected works by Bede and Jerome will be read with specialist advice and Year-1 Latin training. A close reading of Lamentations 4:7-8 against Salters’ commentary (approximately 5,000 words, supplied as a writing sample) demonstrates the method at small scale.

The English investigation has four stages:

- **Old English, c. 700-1150:** lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading of starting points including *Wonders of the East*, Grendel’s eyes and *ælfscȳne*.
- **Middle English, c. 1150-1450:** targeted searches and close reading of biblical, exegetical, encyclopaedic and historical writing, especially Bartholomaeus Anglicus’s *De proprietatibus rerum* (XV.7, "De Albania"), Trevisa’s translation and Isidore’s *Albani*. The Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse and Middle English biblical translations will also be used.
- **c. 1450-1650:** EEBO-TCP provides the principal computational and interpretive corpus for examining reception across religious, literary and natural-historical genres.
- **c. 1650-1914:** a bounded comparison, initially using ECCO and a nineteenth-century collection selected during the pilot, will test later accounts against White’s findings for transmission, transformation, residual influence, parallel development or discontinuity.

"Sacral", "marvellous" and "naturalising" are analytical annotations, not successive stages or mutually exclusive categories. A naturalising account invokes natural processes or physical causes; it need not be medically correct or describe disease, and may coexist with other framings. Separately, descriptions are annotated as potentially ideal, radiant or marking a visibly distinctive body. Age, features, genre, status, reaction, quotation or allusion and uncertainty are also recorded; borderline cases remain open.

Should resources permit, the computational method can be extended to the earlier corpus through sourced/newly-trained models.

### Semantic retrieval and evaluation

The computational component builds on more than 25 years of professional software development, an MSc in Knowledge-based Systems and an existing prototype for provenance-preserving passage retrieval. It uses two search directions: forward searches from early descriptions and imagery, and backward searches from later terms associated with albinism. The paths will be investigated separately before their overlap is assessed.

The encoder under evaluation is MacBERTh, a historically pretrained English language model covering approximately 1450-1950 (Manjavacas and Fonteyn, 2021). Semantic retrieval will be compared with BM25, normalised and lemmatised pattern searches, and hand-built historical lexicons. Hybrid retrieval and contrastive fine-tuning will be considered only if justified by pilot results. Old and Middle English will remain primarily the subject of lexical and collocational searching and close reading, given the model’s training range.

Evaluation uses a hand-annotated gold set drawn from textual anchors, passages identified by White and independent close reading. Precision and recall will be assessed by period and, where possible, genre and feature. The study will identify passages found only by semantic or hybrid retrieval and ask whether they change interpretation or merely add examples. Quotations and biblical echoes will be distinguished from independent use; false positives, corpus bias and overlap between training and searched data will be documented. A prototype query based on a 1972 clinical description of an "albino" child in Malta retrieved the Son of Man description in the English *Apocalyptica*. This is a preliminary lead, not proof of performance, and will be tested against the gold set.

The project uses digital corpora and databases, not human participants; no interviews or personal data collection are planned. Ethical considerations include historical racialised or offensive terminology, representation of people with albinism and the limits of inference from surviving texts. Historical terms will be contextualised. The researcher’s personal experience of "albinism"  informs the questions but does not determine interpretation; evidence, scholarly inference and personal perspective remain distinct. Copyright and corpus licences will govern public release of texts or annotations.
