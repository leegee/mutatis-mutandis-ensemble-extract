# Representing Visible Difference:
## Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Unusual Eyes in English Writing
## c700-1650 with comparisons up to 1914

*Draft PhD research proposal*

### Abstract

This project investigates how English writers from the Old English period to 1914 described and interpreted extraordinarily pale or radiant human and human-like bodies, with particular attention to a durable cluster of biblical imagery drawn from the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:9) and the Son of Man (Revelation 1:13–14). It tests two hypotheses: ~~that prior to the mid-sixteenth century such descriptions predominantly carried numinous, otherworldly or wondrous meanings rooted in biblical and exegetical tradition; and that later naturalistic and medical accounts of bodies associated with albinism drew principally on classical, travel and medical authorities rather than on this earlier repertoire.~~ that before c. 1600 English writing frames extraordinary paleness through two largely separate channels, the sacral (biblical) and the marvellous (ethnographic), with natural explanation rare; and that later naturalistic and medical accounts of bodies associated with albinism drew principally on the marvellous-ethnographic channel and on classical and medical authority, not on the sacral repertoire. Combining close philological and exegetical reading of the earliest layers with a rigorously evaluated, two-ended semantic retrieval method applied to larger early-modern and later corpora, the research recovers a longer English tradition of marked bodily paleness and clarifies what can and cannot be demonstrated about its relationship to the later medical category of albinism. The computational component is deliberately subordinate to historical interpretation and is designed to surface sparse evidence that conventional lexical search may miss. The project contributes to literary history, the history of the body and disability, biblical reception studies, and digital humanities methods for diachronic semantic search.

### 1. Starting point and contribution

Thomas White’s thesis, *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650–1914* (University of Manchester, 2011), traces the changing significance of albinism through travel writing, natural history, medical classification and self-representation. It demonstrates that albinism was not a timeless condition awaiting scientific discovery, but a category whose meanings developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity.

This project examines the same subject across a longer and earlier chronological range. Its central working hypothesis is that a specific cluster of biblical imagery - principally the white hair and radiant appearance of the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9 and the Son of Man in Revelation 1:13–14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition - supplied English writers with a durable repertoire for describing extraordinarily pale or radiant bodies. The research investigates how that repertoire was received, adapted, transformed or displaced in English writing from the Old English period to 1914, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism.

The distinctive contribution is threefold. First, it recovers a much longer English tradition of describing extraordinary bodily paleness and subjects the biblical repertoire to systematic historical testing rather than assuming its influence. Second, it combines close reading of the earliest texts and their exegetical contexts with a bounded, evaluated computational method designed for sparse historical phenomena. Third, it clarifies the limits of demonstrable connection between earlier literary and religious imagery and later naturalistic accounts, thereby refining the historical understanding of how visible bodily difference acquired and lost meanings. The works recovered will be compared and contrasted with those examined by White; the project does not seek to rewrite the post-1650 history he has already provided.

### 2. Central question, hypotheses and research questions

**Central question**

How was a cluster of biblical imagery describing extraordinarily pale or radiant bodies and unusual eyes received, adapted and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to 1914, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism?

Computational retrieval is used as a means of finding evidence that conventional reading may miss, not as an independent research objective. The methodological question (RQ6) is asked in service of the historical one. Two working hypotheses structure the investigation and are deliberately testable and potentially disprovable.

The *marvellous* framing treats pale bodies and peoples as wonders and draws on the ethnographic and paradoxographical tradition (the *Wonders of the East*, Pliny, Solinus, and the Albani of Isidore as received by Bartholomaeus Anglicus and Trevisa). Explanations that give a natural cause for a pale body are rare before 1600 and, where they occur, derive from classical and medical authority rather than from either channel. - **H2: Limited reception into later naturalistic accounts.** Later naturalistic and medical accounts of bodies described in terms associated with albinism ~~draw principally on classical, travel and medical authorities rather than on the earlier biblical, religious and literary imagery.~~ draw principally on the marvellous-ethnographic channel and on classical and medical authority, not on the sacral repertoire. H2 would be challenged by demonstrable chains of quotation, allusion, translation or shared interpretive tradition connecting the two bodies of material. Evidence of both inheritance and discontinuity will be considered.

In H1, *sacral* means that the figure’s paleness signifies divinity, holiness, age or authority within a religious frame; *marvellous* means that the paleness is presented as a wonder or oddity (medieval *admiratio*) without a sacral meaning; *naturalising* means that a cause is offered (descent, climate, humours, maternal imagination). Framing is an annotation field that can take more than one value, and borderline passages stay borderline.

These hypotheses do not presume a continuous tradition. Earlier and later representations may prove largely unrelated; matter-of-fact descriptions may be scarce; and apparent patterns may result from conventional imagery, textual transmission, selection bias or the accidents of survival. The biblical material is treated as a historically available repertoire whose actual influence must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

**Research questions**

1. **Representation and historical change.** How is extraordinary paleness described in English writing, and what meanings attach to it across periods and genres (religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical and medical)? How do its moral and emotional associations vary?

2. **The body and its interpretation.** How do writers distinguish or conflate unusual hair, skin, eyes and bodily radiance, as well as any functions attributed to the eyes (such as better sight at night)? When do these features signify holiness, wonder, danger, monstrosity, physical difference or a condition requiring explanation? What can be learned from descriptions that provoke no explicit reaction?

3. **Historical relationship and discontinuity.** Where can specific connections be demonstrated between earlier representations and later accounts of albinism? What roles do quotation, allusion, translation, shared authorities and genre conventions play? Where does the evidence indicate discontinuity or leave the relationship unresolved?

4. **The English tradition in context.** What do biblical, classical and other sources contribute where a demonstrable channel of reception exists? How do the source-language distinctions among whiteness and brightness, redness and ruddiness compare with their representation in Greek, Latin and subsequent English textual traditions? What can bounded comparison clarify about the specificity (or otherwise) of the English reception of the biblical repertoire?

5. **The afterlife of the sacral white body.** If the biblical repertoire does not pass into naturalistic accounts, where does it go? Possible destinations include devotional language, conventional English descriptions of beautiful bodies, and readings of the white-and-red cluster (Lamentations 4:7, Song of Songs 5:10, 1 Enoch 106) as describing a marked body and not only an ideal one. A negative result for H2 is therefore a finding.

6. **Method.** For a sparse, figurative and lexically unstable concept such as extraordinary paleness, how much historically relevant evidence can concept-level passage retrieval recover that lexical search and lexicon expansion miss? What kinds of evidence does it systematically miss or mislead? What do its failures show about the concept itself? Sub-questions: (a) how do recall and precision differ by period and genre? (b) how far do quotations and biblical echoes drive the results? (c) of the passages found only by the semantic method, how many change a close reading, and how many merely add instances?

### 3. Object of study and scope

#### 3.1 Defining extraordinary paleness and terminology

“Extraordinary paleness” is an analytical term coined for this project, not a historical category assumed to have been recognised consistently. It is preferred to “whiteness” (to avoid confusion with modern racial senses) and to “albinism” (to avoid presupposing a medical category). Historical sources’ own terms, including “white,” “hoary” and “wan,” will be quoted and analysed in context. Outside quotation the project uses “person with albinism” and avoids “albino” as a noun. Later historical terms such as “white negro” and “kakerlak” are racialised or offensive; they will be analysed as evidence of their historical contexts and flagged as such.

The core object comprises descriptions of human or human-like bodies (including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures) in which:

- white or very pale hair occurs alongside at least one further marked bodily feature, such as unusually pale skin or distinctive pale, red or flame-like eyes; or
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a bodily peculiarity in its own right.

Associated features will be recorded separately: bodily luminosity; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor; functions ascribed to the eyes; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; and observers’ reactions. These may illuminate a description without establishing that it belongs to the core object.

Excluded are generic symbolism of white (purity, holiness) when not attached to a described body; similes of snow or wool applied to non-bodies; and descriptions of conventional fair complexion. Borderline cases will be retained in separate categories where excluding them would obscure a potentially significant relationship. Comparative references may be made to animals such as white stags.

Known confounds (“white-headed,” “hoary,” “towhead,” “flaxen” and similar terms) will be managed through lexical work drawing on Biggam’s studies of Old English colour terms, ~~the *Thesaurus of Old English* and the *Historical Thesaurus of English*, together~~ the *Thesaurus of Old English*, the *Historical Thesaurus of English* and etymological reference works (Kluge; Kroonen 2013; Watkins 2000), together with contextual and collocational analysis.

#### 3.2 White hair, brightness, age and markedness

Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13–14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition, form the foundational repertoire whose reception the project investigates. Other texts from the Jewish tradition will be examined for context. (On Daniel 7 see Collins 1993; on Jewish and early Christian apocalyptic, Rowland 1982.) White hair may be associated with age, wisdom, purity, radiance and divine authority, but these associations are neither interchangeable nor self-evident. Patristic interpretations illustrate their plurality: Jerome distinguishes the symbolism of the Ancient of Days’ apparent age from that of his wool-like hair; Origen connects the imagery with heavenly splendour; Cyril of Jerusalem links it to forgiveness and sinlessness via Isaiah 1:18; Bede interprets the whiteness of the head as signifying “the antiquity and immortality of majesty.”

**Worked example: Lamentations 4:7.** The verse describes Jerusalem’s *nəzîrêhā* as whiter than snow and brighter than milk, redder in body than *penînîm*, with a final comparison to sapphire (*sappîr gizratām*). Almost every term is disputed, and the versions diverge. The Septuagint omits *ʿeṣem* (‘body’), and the Vulgate has *rubicundiores ebore antiquo*, “ruddier than old ivory” (the Nova Vulgata of 1979 reads *coralliis*). The Wycliffite Bibles, the earliest surviving complete English renderings of Lamentations (Marsden 2015; Solopova 2016), follow the Vulgate (“reddere þan old yuer” in the Early Version, “rodier than elde yuer” in the Late). The KJV returns to the Hebrew with “rubies” and “their polishing was of Saphir”. Blayney (1784) reads the sapphire as veining (“their veining was the sapphires”), as Braunius did according to Gill (1748–63), and Clarke restates the analogy. R. B. Salters’ ICC commentary (2010) reads the passage as a description of the “refined beauty” of “nobles”, with *gizratām* as lustrous hair.

The researcher’s close reading of Salters (c. 4,900 words) argues that this translation is coherent but not required by the Hebrew. “Nobles” rests on the sense of being set apart, which applies at least as directly to Nazirites. The evidence for hair is no stronger than for veins or the cutting of gems. And verse 8, where the subjects are “not recognised in the streets” once their colours are inverted, is compatible with those colours having been features by which they were known, not only admired. The essay proposes no new translation. It shows where an assumed ideal of beauty shaped the reading of a description whose meaning is uncertain.

Three points for the project. An English reader before 1530 met a human body “whiter than snow” and “ruddier than old ivory” through the Vulgate chain, so the Latin choice shaped the English vocabulary. The Hebrew–Latin gap shows how colour distinctions are recast in translation, which is the question this section sets out to ask. And the verse is a contested case, not a settled boundary one. The same cluster of white, red and perhaps blue can be heard as an ideal complexion (Salters), as a register of radiance, or as a visibly distinctive body. Of the three, the third gives the most direct account of the loss of recognition in verse 8, though the poem does not require it. The nearest parallel, 1 Enoch 106:2–3 (Nickelsburg and VanderKam 2012; Stuckenbruck 2007), calls the new-born Noah “white as snow and red as a rose”, with hair like white wool and eyes that light the house, and his father fears the child is not his and resembles the children of heaven. The annotation scheme therefore keeps “ideal”, “radiant” and “marked” as separate readings and records which one an interpreter assumes. Salters shows how an assumed ideal of beauty can narrow what a physical description is allowed to mean, and the project’s own categories carry the same risk.

#### 3.3 Controls

Controls will test whether apparent patterns are specific to extraordinary paleness or reflect general literary convention. They will include other hair and skin colours (red, black, yellow, green), other bodily extremes (giants, dwarves, the very elderly), and ordinary descriptions of white, hoary and grey hair. The final set will be determined during the pilot. The purpose is interpretive as well as quantitative.

#### 3.4 Corpus and periods

The primary corpus is English writing from the Old English period to 1914, organised into tiers:

1. **Old English, c. 700–1150.** Lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading. Starting points include *Wonders of the East* and the wider marvellous-peoples tradition, Grendel’s eyes, and *ælfscȳne* and related vocabulary. Analysis will follow the philological approach exemplified by Alaric Hall.

2. **Middle English, c. 1150–1450.** Targeted lexical searches, close reading, and an attempt at systematic computational coverage where feasible. Particular attention will be paid to encyclopaedic and historical works, especially Bartholomaeus Anglicus’s *De proprietatibus rerum* (Book XV, chapter 7, “De Albania”) and John Trevisa’s Middle English translation, in order to investigate the reception of descriptions of the *Albani* associated with Isidore of Seville. The University of Michigan’s Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse will be examined, along with Middle English biblical translations, Mandeville’s *Travels*, and selected historical or encyclopaedic works.

3. **c. 1450–1650.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus will be EEBO-TCP. This period is central to investigating how descriptions of the subject were interpreted across genres and whether older religious or literary imagery coexisted with, or was reinterpreted through, emerging natural-historical explanations. The decades from 1550 to 1650 are the zone in which the two channels and naturalising accounts are tested against each other.

4. **c. 1650–1914.** Comparative corpus for the emergence of natural-historical and medical accounts alongside continued literary representation. ECCO will provide the initial resource; a suitable nineteenth-century collection will be selected during the pilot (candidates include HathiTrust, Gale’s nineteenth-century collections, the Wellcome Collection and the *Philosophical Transactions*). Thomas White’s study supplies a starting point for identifying relevant authors and passages; the corpus investigation will test and extend that evidence.

Interpretive effort will be concentrated on Old and Middle English material and the early modern period before 1650. The later corpus serves both as historical comparator and as a test of whether passage-level retrieval can recover material identified independently through existing scholarship.

### 5. Methodology

The technical work builds on the researcher’s background as a professional software developer of more than 25 years and an MSc in Knowledge-based Systems. A working prototype for passage-level retrieval across historical texts has already been developed.

#### 5.1 Design overview

Diachronic semantic search remains an open question (McGillivray et al., 2024). Computational methods are employed here to identify candidate passages in a mass of text too large to survey through reading alone; each discovered passage will be interpreted in relation to its wording, genre, authorship, context and relationship to other sources. The methodological contribution lies in evaluating how existing techniques can support the discovery and interpretation of historically significant evidence for a sparse phenomenon. While individual components have precedents, the specific combination has not, to the researcher’s knowledge, been published.

Closest in spirit are the Linguistic DNA project and the Concept Analytics Lab at Sussex, which model concepts as patterns of paradigmatic relations across large early modern corpora, not as individual words (Allan and Robinson 2012; Linguistic DNA, linguisticdna.org; Robinson \[add specific publication after checking her profile]). The present project differs in starting from a single, lexically unstable and largely figurative concept, and in asking for passage-level evidence that can be read closely. The categories “extraordinary paleness” and “sacral / marvellous / naturalising” are themselves concept-level, so a concept-led method is a natural comparison with passage retrieval on the same gold set. \[Verify the corpus Linguistic DNA used and whether it was EEBO-TCP before stating any overlap.]

#### 5.2 Two-ended retrieval

- **Forward from bodily description.** Queries begin with early anchors and related descriptions (“heeris weren whyte as whyte wolle and as snow,” “eyes like fire,” radiance). Retrieved passages inform subsequent queries.
- **Backward from the later category.** Searches begin with passages containing *albino* and historical vocabulary that preceded or accompanied it (“white negro,” *leucaethiops*, Dondos, *kakerlak* and variants), then expand through contextual vocabulary.

The two paths will be investigated separately before comparison; their overlap is of particular value.

#### 5.3 Passage-level retrieval and annotation

Retrieval operates on dynamic embeddings at multiple scales (individual tokens and overlapping word windows). Each passage retains bibliographic provenance and location. The annotation scheme distinguishes hair, skin, eyes, radiance and ascribed eye functions; co-occurrence or independence of features; ~~age cues and other explanations; the figure’s status;~~ age cues and other explanations; framing (sacral, marvellous or naturalising, with more than one value allowed); the reading of colour descriptions as ideal, radiant or marked; the figure’s status; genre and discourse; recorded reactions; quotation, allusion or independent use; and confidence/interpretive uncertainty. Borderline examples will be retained rather than forced into binary classification. The scheme will be piloted and revised in Year 1.

#### 5.4 Models, baselines and pilot evidence

The central methodological question is the extent to which iterative semantic retrieval can discover relevant historical evidence that conventional lexical methods miss. Preliminary work has already shown that a 1972 clinical description of an “albino” child in Malta surfaces the description of the Son of Man in the *English Apocalyptica* (documented at https://github.com/leegee/mutatis-mutandis-passage-search). The prototype has also retrieved Middle English biblical material, including Wycliffite renderings of Revelation 1:14, which predate MacBERTh’s principal training range - an encouraging but limited lead that will be tested systematically against a pre-1450 gold-set subset.

The encoder under evaluation is ~~MacBERTh (Manjavacas & Fonteyn, 2022)~~ MacBERTh (Manjavacas & Fonteyn, 2021), a BERT-base model pre-trained on English from approximately 1450 to 1950. The current prototype derives contextual representations from token- and document-level processing of chunks up to 510 subword tokens, with passage representations formed as the unweighted mean of vectors for 40-word windows (20-word hop). Layer-eight, final-layer and mean-of-last-four-layer vectors are implemented, with optional post-processing (corpus-mean centring, whitening).

Evaluation will compare:

1. Lexical baseline (BM25, pattern matching over normalised and lemmatised text, hand-built historical lexicons).
2. MacBERTh representations (multiple layers, with and without post-processing).
3. Contrastively fine-tuned MacBERTh (if justified by pilot results; held-out evaluation only).
4. Hybrid retrieval (lexical candidate generation followed by dense or fine-tuned re-ranking).

Relevance will be assessed in historical context rather than inferred from semantic similarity alone. Precision and recall will be measured against a hand-annotated gold set built from known anchors, passages identified by White, and independent close reading. Plausible but misleading matches will be included in the assessment. Potential overlap between MacBERTh’s pre-training data and the corpora searched will be documented. Old and Middle English will remain primarily a matter of lexical and collocational search and close reading. RQ6 is operationalised as recall at a fixed precision against the gold set, the share of gold-set passages found only by the dense or hybrid method, and the number of unique finds that close reading judges interpretively significant. It tests concept-first (onomasiological) search against word-first search on a case where the words are unstable.

#### 5.5 Status of the method (summary)

A working prototype already supports Postgres ingestion from TEI and plain text, document-context processing, seed-token neighbour retrieval and clustering, multiple passage-window configurations, LanceDB indexing, date filtering and query-by-example. A FastAPI backend and SolidJS interface enable passage inspection with source provenance. Lexical baselines, general-purpose sentence embeddings, contrastive fine-tuning and the nineteenth-century corpus selection remain Year-1 tasks. The annotated sample is itself a research output.

#### 5.6 Quotation, reception and validation

Quotations are treated as evidence of reception. Results will distinguish searches that include quotations from those that exclude them. A hand-annotated sample will assess precision by period (and, where sample sizes permit, by feature and genre). Recall will be assessed against the gold set. Where feasible a second annotator will assess a subset; limits of agreement will be reported. False positives and borderline cases will be analysed as evidence about both the method and the definition of the object.

The surviving record may preserve unusual encounters precisely because they provoked a response. The project will distinguish whether a text records a reaction from the kind of reaction recorded, and will give particular attention to matter-of-fact descriptions as a counterpoint to symbolic or sensational accounts. Frequency of recorded reactions can be measured in the surviving material; the probability that an ordinary encounter was recorded cannot.

### 7. Risks and limitations

- **Breadth.** Mitigation: explicit core/contextual/excluded distinction (Section 3.5), Year-1 review, ~~and a comparative chapter that can be reduced.~~ and comparative material that can be reduced.
- **Method performance.** MacBERTh may not outperform a lexical baseline. Mitigation: comparative baselines and, if useful, a hybrid design; the thesis’s claims will rest on close reading, with retrieval serving as support.
- **Sparse evidence.** Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, cautious lexicon expansion and close reading where counts are low.
- **Corpus and OCR bias.** EEBO-TCP is hand-keyed; later material may contain OCR errors. MacBERTh’s training set includes EEBO and ECCO material, which helps mitigate orthographic and OCR noise; residual limitations will be documented.
- **Unstable object.** Age-related whiteness and core/associated feature distinctions may remain ambiguous. Mitigation: borderline categories and sensitivity tests.
- **False continuity.** Similar descriptions may be mistaken for transmission. Mitigation: separate resemblance from documented reception; leave unresolved relationships unresolved.
- **Circularity.** Seed descriptions shape what the system finds. Mitigation: two-ended retrieval, controls, analysis of false positives and validation through close reading.
- **Limits of the record.** Texts cannot show how representative their depictions were of ordinary encounters.
- **Language competence.** The researcher has some competency in scriptural Hebrew, Modern German and Scandinavian languages, Old and Middle English, and is comfortable with specialist editions of Germanic languages. ~~Specialist advice will be sought where necessary, particularly for Latin and the ancient Semitic languages, and for Old Welsh and Old Irish.~~ Latin is addressed by a bounded scope and Year-1 training (Section 3.2). Specialist advice will be sought where necessary for the ancient Semitic languages and for Old Welsh and Old Irish.

### 8. Impact and beneficiaries

The research will be relevant to people with albinism and their families, educators, and organisations concerned with albinism and minority representation. No reliable UK-wide epidemiological prevalence figure has been established; by comparison, Kromberg, Flynn and Kerr (2023) report a mean rate of 1 in 13,000 in the three European countries with adequate large-sample studies. By investigating a wider historical range of bodily descriptions, the project will assess whether the surviving record complicates or challenges predominantly negative contemporary accounts of this substantial but often unseen minority.

Findings will be communicated in accessible language. Opportunities will be explored to consult organisations such as Standing Voice and national albinism support groups on the presentation and dissemination of results. Academic beneficiaries include scholars of literary history, the history of the body and disability, biblical reception, and digital humanities methods for sparse historical phenomena. The reproducible retrieval pipeline and annotated sample (rights permitting) will constitute reusable resources.

### 9. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who has lived with albinism for over fifty years. This informs the choice of questions and an awareness of the consequences of historical representation, but does not determine the meanings found in the evidence. The project does not assume that albinism or extraordinary paleness has an inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that writers, communities and traditions attached to visibly marked bodies. Evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher’s own judgement will be kept distinct.
