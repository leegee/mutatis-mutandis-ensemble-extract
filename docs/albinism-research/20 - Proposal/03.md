# Representing Visible Difference:
## Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Unusual Eyes in English Writing
## c. 700-1650, with comparative evidence to 1914

*Draft PhD research proposal*

### Abstract

This project investigates how English writers from the Old English period to c. 1650 described and interpreted extraordinarily pale or radiant human and human-like bodies, with particular attention to a distinctive biblical repertoire associated with the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:9) and the Son of Man (Revelation 1:13-14). Its principal concern is the reception and transformation of this sacral representation: how white hair, radiance and unusual eyes acquired meaning through scripture, Latin exegesis, vernacular translation and English literary writing. It asks how far this repertoire can be traced across periods and genres, how it differs from other accounts of extraordinary bodies, and what becomes of it in later writing. A bounded comparative investigation of material from c. 1650 to 1914 will examine how representations of extraordinary palidity relate to the earlier traditions identified in this study, and will assess their continuity, transformation, residual influence or discontinuity in the period covered by Thomas White's research on the history of albinism. It will not attempt a comprehensive history of albinism in its own right. Close philological and exegetical reading of the foundational texts is combined with an evaluated, two-ended semantic retrieval method applied to larger early-modern and later corpora. The computational component is subordinate to historical interpretation and is designed to recover sparse evidence that conventional lexical search may miss. The project contributes to literary history, the history of the body and disability, biblical reception studies, and digital humanities methods for diachronic semantic search.

### 1. Starting point and contribution

Thomas White’s thesis, *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650-1914* (University of Manchester, 2011), traces the changing significance of albinism through travel writing, natural history, medical classification and self-representation. It demonstrates that albinism was not a timeless condition awaiting scientific discovery, but a category whose meanings developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity.

This project examines the subject across a longer and earlier chronological range, with its principal historical investigation running from c. 700 to 1650. Its central working hypothesis is that the biblical descriptions of the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9 and the Son of Man in Revelation 1:13-14, together with their Latin exegetical and vernacular reception, supplied English writers with a distinctive and potentially durable repertoire for representing extraordinary whiteness and radiance. The project investigates how that sacral repertoire was received, adapted, transformed, combined with other ways of representing unusual bodies, or displaced. A comparative investigation of writing from c. 1650 to 1914 asks what, if anything, survives of the earlier repertoire in later descriptions associated with albinism; it does not seek to reproduce the comprehensive post-1650 history provided by White.

The distinctive contribution is threefold. First, it reconstructs the reception of a sacral repertoire of extraordinary whiteness in English writing, testing rather than assuming its influence. Second, it distinguishes demonstrable transmission from resemblance, parallel development and later transformation by setting the biblical material alongside a limited range of marvellous and naturalising accounts. Third, it combines close philological and exegetical reading with a bounded, evaluated computational method designed for sparse historical phenomena. The later comparative material will be read alongside the evidence assembled by White, not as an attempt to rewrite the post-1650 history he has already provided.

### 2. Central question, hypotheses and research questions

**Central question**

How was the biblical and exegetical representation of extraordinary palidity and radiance received, adapted and interpreted in English writing from c. 700 to 1650, and what continuities, transformations or discontinuities can be traced in comparative material from c. 1650 to 1914?

The project is centred on the sacral representation of the extraordinary pale body. Other traditions are considered where they provide necessary comparison or help establish whether a later text reflects transmission, resemblance or independent development. Computational retrieval is used to find evidence that conventional reading may miss, not as an independent research objective. The methodological question (RQ6) is asked in service of the historical investigation. The hypotheses are deliberately testable and do not assume an unbroken tradition.

- **H1: The reception of a sacral repertoire.** The biblical descriptions of extraordinary whiteness and radiance, particularly Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition, provide a recognisable repertoire available to English writers from the Old English period onwards. The project expects to find this repertoire received and reinterpreted in more than one genre and period, but does not assume that every description of pale hair, skin or eyes derives from it. H1 would be weakened if the anticipated English reception proved slight, if apparent parallels could not be connected to the biblical or exegetical tradition, or if the relevant features were better explained by independent conventions.

- **H2: The afterlife and transformation of the sacral white body.** In later writing, including accounts from c. 1650-1914 associated with albinism, elements of the earlier sacral repertoire may be transmitted, adapted, secularised, combined with other traditions, survive as residual associations, or disappear. The project does not presuppose that later naturalistic accounts either inherit or reject the earlier repertoire. H2 will be assessed through demonstrable evidence such as quotation, allusion, translation, shared authorities, genre conventions and distinctive clusters of description. Discontinuity, parallel development and unresolved relationships remain possible findings.

The project distinguishes representational framing from the interpretation of physical features. *Sacral* describes a religious frame in which paleness or radiance signifies divinity, holiness, age or authority. *Marvellous* describes the presentation of a body or people as a wonder or oddity (*admiratio*), without necessarily assigning it a sacral meaning. _Naturalising_ describes an account that explains a bodily characteristic by reference to natural processes or physical causes, rather than treating it solely as a sign of divine agency or as a marvel. Such accounts might invoke inheritance, environment or bodily constitution, where these explanations occur in the sources. A naturalising explanation need not be medically correct, need not describe a disease, and may coexist with sacral or marvellous framing. These are analytical annotations, not mutually exclusive historical stages, so a passage may receive more than one value, and uncertain cases will remain. Separately, the project records whether a description is interpreted as an ideal, as radiant, or as marking a visibly distinctive body. These interpretative possibilities may overlap and will not be treated as equivalent to the framing categories.

The hypotheses do not presume a continuous tradition: earlier and later representations may prove largely unrelated; mundane, matter-of-fact descriptions may be scarce; and apparent patterns may result from conventional imagery, textual transmission, selection bias or the accidents of survival. The biblical material is treated as a historically available repertoire whose actual influence must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

**Research questions**

1. **The sacral repertoire and its reception.** How are the descriptions (eg Lamentations 4:9, Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14) interpreted in their source-language, Latin exegetical and vernacular contexts? Which physical features and symbolic associations are retained, altered or omitted as the imagery enters English writing?

2. **Representation of the body.** How do English writers describe and distinguish unusual hair, skin, eyes and bodily radiance? What meanings are attached to these features, and how do age, status, genre, religious context and the reactions of observers affect their interpretation? What can be learned from descriptions that provoke no explicit reaction?

3. **Comparison and distinction.** Where do marvellous, ethnographic or naturalising accounts resemble the sacral repertoire, and where can a channel of reception be demonstrated? How do writers combine, distinguish or confuse these ways of representing unusual bodies?

4. **Transformation and afterlife.** How are elements of the sacral repertoire transmitted, adapted, secularised, combined with other traditions or displaced in English writing, particularly in the comparative period c. 1650-1914? What evidence supports continuity, residual influence, parallel development or discontinuity?

5. **The wider textual context.** How do source-language distinctions among whiteness and brightness, redness and ruddiness compare with their representation in intermediary languages and subsequent English textual traditions? What can bounded comparison with selected Jewish, classical and vernacular material clarify about the specificity of the English reception?

6. **Method.** For a sparse, figurative and lexically unstable concept such as extraordinary paleness, how much historically relevant evidence can concept-level passage retrieval recover that lexical search and lexicon expansion miss? What kinds of evidence does it systematically miss or misrepresent? Sub-questions: 
	1. How do recall and precision differ by period and genre?
	2. How far do quotations and biblical echoes drive the results? 
	3. Of the passages found only by the semantic method, how many change a close reading, and how many merely add instances?

### 3. Object of study and scope

#### 3.1 Defining extraordinary paleness and terminology

"Extraordinary paleness" is an analytical term coined for this project, not a historical category assumed to have been recognised consistently. It is preferred to "whiteness" (to avoid confusion with modern racial senses) and to "albinism" (to avoid presupposing a medical category). Historical sources’ own terms, including "white," "hoary" and "wan," will be quoted and analysed in context. Outside quotation the project uses "person with albinism" and avoids "albino" as a noun. Several later historical terms are racialised or offensive; they will be analysed as evidence of their historical contexts and flagged as such.

The core object comprises descriptions of human or human-like bodies (including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures) in which:

- white or very pale hair occurs alongside at least one further marked bodily feature, such as unusually pale skin or distinctive pale, red or flame-like eyes; bright-white or molten feet; or
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a bodily peculiarity in its own right.

Associated features will be recorded separately: bodily luminosity; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor; functions ascribed to the eyes; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; and observers’ reactions. These may illuminate a description without establishing that it belongs to the core object.

Excluded are instances of generic symbolism of white (purity, holiness) when not attached to a described body, and similes of snow or wool applied to non-bodies. Conventional descriptions of fair complexion are excluded where they occur independently of the features under investigation. However, descriptions in which an unusual white/red pairing appears to be attenuated, conventionalised or re-expressed as blonde or fair hair and pink, rosy or ruddy complexion will be retained where they may illuminate the transformation or loss of an earlier representational pattern.

Known confounds ("white-headed," "hoary," "towhead," "flaxen" and similar terms) will be managed through lexical work drawing on Biggam’s studies of Old English colour terms,  the *Thesaurus of Old English*, the *Historical Thesaurus of English* and etymological reference works (Kluge; Kroonen 2013; Watkins 2000), together with contextual and collocational analysis.

#### 3.2 The biblical repertoire: whiteness, brightness, age and markedness

Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13–14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition, form the foundational repertoire whose reception the project investigates. This material is substantial and foundational enough to warrant a standalone chapter (Chapter 2). White hair may be associated with age, wisdom, purity, radiance, fear and/or divine authority, but these associations are neither interchangeable nor self-evident. Patristic interpretations illustrate their plurality: Jerome distinguishes the symbolism of the Ancient of Days’ apparent age from that of his wool-like hair; Origen connects the imagery with heavenly splendour; Cyril of Jerusalem links it to forgiveness and sinlessness via Isaiah 1:18; Bede interprets the whiteness of the head as signifying “the antiquity and immortality of majesty.”

The project will trace the reception of this imagery in English apocalyptic writing, including vernacular versions and interpretations of Revelation represented in the English _Apocalyptica_. These texts provide direct evidence for how the biblical description was transmitted into English, and for what happened to its vocabulary and associations in the process. Particular attention will be paid to the retention, alteration or loss of the language of wool, snow, whiteness and radiance, and to whether the figure's extraordinary appearance continues to carry recognisably sacral associations. Selected illustrated English Apocalypse manuscripts, including the Trinity and Bohun Apocalypses, will provide additional visual context for the textual descriptions. This is not intended as a systematic iconographic survey: the principal concern remains the textual reception and interpretation of the imagery.

Two further Jewish passages provide important context for the repertoire, though in different ways. Lamentations 4:7 is the earliest example identified so far in the project's preliminary research of a striking combination of whiteness, brightness and redness in a description of human appearance. Almost every term in the passage is disputed, and its precise meaning remains uncertain; nevertheless, it offers an important point of comparison for the colour language examined in this project. In 1 Enoch 106:2–3, the newborn Noah is described as white as snow and red as a rose, with hair like white wool and eyes that illuminate the house. His father's alarm at the child's appearance, and his suspicion that the child may have a heavenly rather than human origin, connect extraordinary colouring with the marvellous, supernatural and potentially divine. Without claiming direct influence on Revelation, this passage demonstrates that a closely related cluster of imagery was present in Jewish apocalyptic tradition before the emergence of the Christian Apocalypse.

The Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek source languages serve as a baseline for what the Vulgate and later English renderings recast; they are not claimed as a channel of transmission, since English readers met these verses through Latin and then English. The baseline is limited to the passages where colour vocabulary matters (Daniel 7:9, Song of Songs 5:10 and Lamentations 4:7). In Biblical Hebrew the root *l-b-n* (לבן) is associated with whiteness but is not interchangeable with *ṣaḥ* (צח) (brightness/radiance) or *šeleḡ* (שלג) (snow). These will be considered alongside vocabulary for redness and ruddiness (*ʾādōm*), and the rare and disputed *pāʾrūr*. Daniel 7:9 is in Aramaic and uses *ḥiwwār* (see Sokoloff 1976 on the wool comparison, and Jastrow and Sokoloff’s dictionaries for the vocabulary). The analysis will examine how these distinctions are retained, recast or lost in the Greek versions and subsequent Latin tradition, and how the choices affect interpretation of bodily appearance. Revelation 1:14 is Greek translated into Latin, so Hebrew gives no access to it. Each relevant passage will be annotated for explicit or implicit age cues, explicit youth, or the absence of a discernible age cue.

**Languages and sources.** The project is about vernacular English. Medieval English biblical and encyclopaedic writing is largely mediated by Latin, so the project works with Latin at three bounded levels. First, a short set of passages is read in the original and checked with a Latinist: the Vulgate of Daniel 7:9, Revelation 1:13-14, Song of Songs 5:10, Lamentations 4:7 and Isaiah 1:18, and the Albani passages in Isidore (*Etymologiae* IX.2.65) and Bartholomaeus (XV.7), read against Trevisa. Second, the Latin commentaries (Bede, Jerome, Origen) are read in published translation, with key terms checked in the Latin. Third, Hebrew and Aramaic serve as a baseline, as described above. The researcher’s close reading of Lamentations 4:7-8 against Salters’ commentary (c. 4,900 words, available as a writing sample) shows this method at small scale.

**Worked example: Lamentations 4:7.** The verse describes Jerusalem’s *nəzîrêhā* as whiter than snow and brighter than milk, redder in body than *penînîm*, with a final comparison to sapphire (*sappîr gizratām*). Almost every term is disputed, and the versions diverge. The Septuagint omits *ʿeṣem* (‘body’), and the Vulgate has *rubicundiores ebore antiquo*, "ruddier than old ivory" (the Nova Vulgata of 1979 reads *coralliis*). The Wycliffite Bibles, the earliest surviving complete English renderings of Lamentations (Marsden 2015; Solopova 2016), follow the Vulgate ("reddere þan old yuer" in the Early Version, "rodier than elde yuer" in the Late). The KJV seems to reread the Hebrew to get "rubies" and "their polishing was of Saphir". Blayney (1784) reads the sapphire as veining ("their veining was the sapphires"), as Braunius did according to Gill (1748-63), and Clarke restates the analogy. R. B. Salters’ ICC commentary (2010) reads the passage as a description of the "refined beauty" of "nobles", with *gizratām* as lustrous hair.

The researcher’s close reading of Salters (c. 5,000 words) argues that this translation is coherent but not required by the Hebrew. "Nobles" rests on the sense of being set apart, which applies at least as directly to Nazirites. The evidence for hair is no stronger than for veins or the cutting of gems. Verse 8, where the subjects are "not recognised in the streets" once their colours are inverted, is compatible with those colours having been features by which they were known, not only admired. The essay proposes no new translation. It shows where an assumed ideal of beauty shaped the reading of a description whose meaning is uncertain.

The example matters to the project in three ways. An English reader before 1530 met a human body "whiter than snow ... ruddier than old ivory" through the Vulgate chain, so the Latin choice shaped the English vocabulary and potentially imagination. The Hebrew–Latin gap shows how colour distinctions are recast in translation. The same cluster of white, red and perhaps blue can be heard as an ideal complexion (Salters), as a register of radiance, a marker of divinity, or as a visibly distinctive body. The annotation scheme therefore records "ideal", "radiant" and "marked" as potentially overlapping interpretations rather than treating any one as the default. Salters shows how an assumed ideal of beauty can narrow what a physical description is allowed to mean, and the project’s own categories carry the same risk.

**Visual context.** Selected illustrated English Apocalypse manuscripts, including the Trinity and Bohun Apocalypses, will be considered briefly to establish the visual setting in which the Son of Man description was transmitted and encountered. This is a contextual investigation, not a systematic iconographic survey; where relevant, it will ask whether the images appear to reflect the text’s description of white hair or instead follow established conventions for depicting sacred figures (Lewis 1995; Emmerson and Lewis 1984-86).

#### 3.3 Controls

Controls will test whether apparent patterns are specific to extraordinary paleness or reflect general literary convention. They will include other hair and skin colours (red, black, yellow, green), other bodily extremes (giants, dwarves, the very elderly), and ordinary descriptions of white, hoary and grey hair. The final set will be determined during the pilot. The purpose is interpretive as well as quantitative.

#### 3.4 Corpus and periods

The principal historical investigation is English writing from c. 700 to 1650. Material from c. 1650 to 1914 forms a bounded comparative period, used to test the later reception and transformation of the earlier repertoire rather than to provide a second survey of equal scope.

1. **Old English, c. 700-1150.** Lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading. Starting points include *Wonders of the East* and the wider marvellous-peoples tradition, Grendel’s eyes, and *ælfscȳne* and related vocabulary. The main question is how extraordinary bodily appearance is represented in English texts and how those representations relate to, or differ from, the sacral repertoire. Analysis will follow the philological approach exemplified by Alaric Hall. A limited Old Norse comparison may be used where it clarifies a specific contrast, but it will not become a separate comparative strand.

2. **Middle English, c. 1150-1450.** Targeted lexical searches, close reading, and an attempt at systematic computational coverage where feasible. Particular attention will be paid to biblical and exegetical writing and to encyclopaedic and historical works, especially Bartholomaeus Anglicus’s *De proprietatibus rerum* (Book XV, chapter 7, "De Albania") and John Trevisa’s Middle English translation, in order to investigate the reception of descriptions of the *Albani* associated with Isidore of Seville. The University of Michigan’s Corpus of Middle English Prose and Verse will be examined, along with Middle English biblical translations, Mandeville’s *Travels*, and selected historical or encyclopaedic works.

3. **c. 1450-1650.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus will be EEBO-TCP. This period is central to investigating how the sacral repertoire is received across genres, how it coexists or interacts with marvellous descriptions, and whether or how naturalising explanations emerge. The decades from 1550 to 1650 will be treated as a transition and testing zone, not as a sharp boundary between fixed historical stages.

4. **c. 1650-1914 (comparative).** A bounded comparative corpus will examine natural-historical and medical accounts alongside selected literary representations. ECCO will provide the initial resource; a suitable nineteenth-century collection will be selected during the pilot (candidates include HathiTrust, Gale’s nineteenth-century collections, the Wellcome Collection and the *Philosophical Transactions*). Thomas White’s study supplies a starting point for identifying relevant authors and passages; the corpus investigation will test and extend that evidence. The purpose is to investigate whether earlier sacral imagery is transmitted, transformed, secularised, retained residually, combined with other traditions or absent—not to survey all later representations of albinism.

Interpretive effort will be concentrated on the biblical and exegetical foundation, Old and Middle English material, and the early modern period before 1650. The later corpus is smaller in interpretive ambition and serves as a historical comparator and a test of whether passage-level retrieval can recover material identified independently through existing scholarship.

Should time and resources permit, the researcher's existingcomputational methodology will also be applied to the earlier corpora.

#### 3.5 Core, contextual and excluded material

The project is deliberately bounded. A three-year doctorate cannot provide complete coverage of all traditions that touch this topic. The hierarchy below gives priority to the sacral repertoire and its English reception.

**Core (committed)**
- Biblical and early exegetical repertoire (Lamentations 4, Daniel 7,  Revelation 1), including the bounded Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek and Latin textual comparisons described in Section 3.2.
- A standalone chapter on the biblical imagery and its exegetical and translational history.
- Old English close reading and targeted lexical/collocational work.
- Middle English reception, especially biblical, encyclopaedic and historical writing.
- Early-modern retrieval and close reading to 1650.
- A bounded comparative investigation of c. 1650-1914 (potentially earlier), including backward searches from later vocabulary associated with albinism.
- Assessment of transmission, transformation, residual influence, parallel development and discontinuity.

**Contextual (bounded, in service of the core)**
- Limited apocryphal comparison (especially 1 Enoch 106), traced only where relevant to the interpretation of the biblical colour cluster and through demonstrable English channels where reception is claimed.
- Limited classical ethnography and paradoxography (Pliny, Solinus on the Albani and *leucaethiopes*), considered where it helps distinguish the marvellous tradition from the sacral repertoire or where reception in English can be shown.
- Limited Latin and Anglo-Norman writing from England as a small control set (e.g. the Green Children of Woolpit  in William of Newburgh's *Historia rerum Anglicarum,* c. 1189 and Ralph of Coggeshall's *Chronicum Anglicanum,* c. 1220).
- Selected Apocalypse manuscript illustrations as visual context for the transmission of Revelation 1, not as a systematic iconographic study.
- A small Old Norse, Irish and Welsh comparison where it clarifies a specific question about the representation of pale or radiant bodies in a non-biblical Germanic setting.

**Not undertaken**
Systematic coverage of non-English originals; computational coverage of Old and Middle English treated as equivalent to later periods; a second general history of albinism after 1650; a systematic survey of Welsh and Irish traditions; a systematic survey of manuscript iconography; a survey of the twentieth century.

**Review point**
At the end of Year 1 the productivity and scope of the biblical-reception hypothesis will be assessed. The scale of the Middle English and comparative components will be fixed on the basis of pilot results. Anything infeasible will be dropped rather than compressed.

### 4. Existing scholarship

White’s thesis remains the principal point of departure for the history of albinism as a category after 1650. The present project begins with a much earlier set of bodily descriptions and asks how the biblical and exegetical representation of extraordinary whiteness was received in English writing, and whether any relationship to later accounts can be demonstrated.

Scholarship on biblical textual transmission, the Latin exegetical tradition (especially Bede’s *Expositio Apocalypseos*), and historical colour terminology (Hartley, Németh, Biggam, Viti) provides the foundation for the earliest layers. Alaric Hall’s *Elves in Anglo-Saxon England* offers a framework for supernatural beings, radiance and unusual bodies in Old English. Work on wonder and the marvellous provides a bounded comparative framework for distinguishing other representations of unusual bodies from the sacral repertoire. Jackson Crawford’s work on basic colour terms in Old Norse-Icelandic supplies a comparative resource if required.

 The conceptual framework is organised by question and not by school. How an image passes from scripture into English writing is approached through figural and typological reading (Auerbach, Frye, Fabiny) and through the medieval exegetical and translation tradition (Minnis, Copeland, Poleg). How a repertoire persists, fades or is displaced is approached through Raymond Williams’s distinction between residual, dominant and emergent cultural forms, Ricoeur’s account of how metaphor sediments into convention, and Koselleck’s distinction between a word and a concept, which keeps changes in vocabulary separate from changes in interpretive frame. Wonder and the marvellous are approached through Daston and Park, Bynum and Greenblatt’s *Marvelous Possessions*. Because white-and-red descriptions of bodies are entangled with later racial thought, the project engages medieval and early modern critical race scholarship (Heng, Whitaker, Hall) without assuming either that the medieval material is already racial or that it is not. Cultural materialist and New Historicist work (Dollimore, Greenblatt’s *Renaissance Self-Fashioning*) bears on the blazon and on ideology in the early modern chapter.

Work in critical disability studies and African literary studies  has examined the stigma attached to albinism in other cultural contexts (for example Baker et al. 2010). The present project remains focused on the English literary and historical record.

### 5. Methodology

The computational work builds on the researcher’s background as a professional software developer of more than 25 years and an MSc in Knowledge-based Systems. A working prototype for passage-level retrieval across historical texts has already been developed.

#### 5.1 Design overview

Diachronic semantic search remains an open question (McGillivray et al., 2024). Computational methods are employed here to identify candidate passages in a mass of text too large to survey through reading alone; each discovered passage will be interpreted in relation to its wording, genre, authorship, context and relationship to other sources. The methodological contribution lies in evaluating how existing techniques can support the discovery and interpretation of historically significant evidence for a sparse phenomenon. While individual components have precedents, the specific combination has not, to the researcher’s knowledge, been published.

Closest in spirit are the Linguistic DNA project and the Concept Analytics Lab at Sussex, which model concepts as patterns of paradigmatic relations across large early modern corpora, not as individual words (Allan and Robinson 2012; Linguistic DNA, linguisticdna.org; Robinson [XXX ADD CITE]). The present project differs in starting from a single, lexically unstable and largely figurative concept, and in asking for passage-level evidence that can be read closely. The category "extraordinary paleness" is itself concept-level; the framing labels (sacral, marvellous and naturalising) are annotation dimensions used to interpret retrieved passages, not separate concepts that the search system is expected to classify automatically, though preliminary work with LLMs has proved encouraging. \[TODO Verify the corpus Linguistic DNA used and whether it was EEBO-TCP before stating any overlap.]

#### 5.2 Two-ended retrieval

- **Forward from bodily description.** Queries begin with early anchors and related descriptions ("heeris weren whyte as whyte wolle and as snow," "eyes like fire," radiance). Retrieved passages inform subsequent queries.
- **Backward from the later category.** Searches begin with passages containing *albino* and historical vocabulary that preceded or accompanied it ("white negro," *leucaethiops*, Dondos, *kakerlak* and variants), then expand through contextual vocabulary.

The two paths will be investigated separately before comparison; their overlap is of particular value.

#### 5.3 Passage-level retrieval and annotation

Retrieval operates on dynamic embeddings at multiple scales (individual tokens and overlapping word windows). Each passage retains bibliographic provenance and location. The annotation scheme distinguishes hair, skin, eyes, radiance and ascribed eye functions; co-occurrence or independence of features; age cues; framing (sacral, marvellous and/or naturalising); any causal explanation offered, without assuming that it is in any way medically accurate or that it presents a disease; the reading of colour descriptions as ideal, radiant and/or marked; the figure’s status; genre and discourse; recorded reactions; quotation, allusion or independent use; and confidence/interpretive uncertainty. Borderline examples will be retained rather than forced into binary classification. The scheme will be piloted and revised in Year 1.

#### 5.4 Models, baselines and pilot evidence

The central methodological question is the extent to which semantic retrieval, iterative or otherwise, can discover relevant historical evidence that conventional lexical methods miss. Preliminary work has already shown that a 1972 clinical description of an "albino" child in Malta surfaces the description of the Son of Man in the *English Apocalyptica* (documented at github.com/leegee/mutatis-mutandis-passage-search). The same query has also retrieved Middle English biblical material, including Wycliffite renderings of Revelation 1:14, which predate MacBERTh’s principal training range - an encouraging but limited lead that will be tested systematically against a pre-1450 gold-set subset.

The encoder under evaluation is MacBERTh (Manjavacas & Fonteyn, 2021), a BERT-base model pre-trained on English from approximately 1450 to 1950. The current prototype derives contextual representations from token- and document-level processing of chunks up to 510 subword tokens, with passage representations formed as the unweighted mean of vectors for 40-word windows (20-word hop). Layer-eight, final-layer and mean-of-last-four-layer vectors are implemented, with optional post-processing (corpus-mean centring, whitening).

Evaluation will compare:

1. Lexical baseline (BM25, pattern matching over normalised and lemmatised text, hand-built historical lexicons).
2. MacBERTh representations (multiple layers, with and without post-processing).
3. Contrastively fine-tuned MacBERTh (if justified by pilot results; held-out evaluation only).
4. Hybrid retrieval (lexical candidate generation followed by dense or fine-tuned re-ranking).

Relevance will be assessed in historical context rather than inferred from semantic similarity alone. Precision and recall will be measured against a hand-annotated gold set built from known anchors, passages identified by White, and independent close reading. Plausible but misleading matches will be included in the assessment. Potential overlap between MacBERTh’s pre-training data and the corpora searched will be documented. 

Old and Middle English will remain primarily a matter of lexical and collocational search and close reading. If time and resources permit, further models could by sourced/trained and linked into the iterative diachronic search mechanism without the need for vector alignment.

RQ6 is operationalised as recall at a fixed precision against the gold set, the share of gold-set passages found only by the dense or hybrid method, and the number of unique finds that close reading judges interpretively significant. It tests concept-first (onomasiological) search against word-first search on a case where the words are unstable.

#### 5.5 Status of the method (summary)

A working prototype already supports Postgres ingestion from TEI and plain text, document-context processing, seed-token neighbour retrieval and clustering, multiple passage-window configurations, LanceDB indexing, date filtering and query-by-example. Both a command-line interface and a FastAPI backend with a SolidJS interface share a common API and core to enable passage inspection with source provenance. Hooks exist for LLM and agentic intervention both for search and tentative classification of (clustered) results, with promising initial results.  Lexical baselines, general-purpose sentence embeddings, contrastive fine-tuning and the nineteenth-century corpus selection remain Year-1 tasks. The annotated sample is itself a research output.

#### 5.6 Quotation, reception and validation

Quotations are treated as evidence of reception. Results will distinguish searches that include quotations from those that exclude them. A hand-annotated sample will assess precision by period (and, where sample sizes permit, by feature and genre). Recall will be assessed against the gold set. Where feasible a second annotator will assess a subset; limits of agreement will be reported. False positives and borderline cases will be analysed as evidence about both the method and the definition of the object.

The surviving record may preserve unusual encounters precisely because they provoked a response. The project will distinguish whether a text records a reaction from the kind of reaction recorded, and will give particular attention to matter-of-fact descriptions as a counterpoint to symbolic or sensational accounts. Frequency of recorded reactions can be measured in the surviving material; the probability that an ordinary encounter was recorded cannot.

### 6. Chapter structure, outputs and three-year plan

**Provisional chapter structure**

1. **Introduction:** object, terminology, central question, scope, scholarship and method. Establishes the project’s focus on the sacral repertoire and explains the role of comparative material and semantic retrieval.
2. **The biblical white body:** Lamentations 4:7, Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14, 1 Enoch 106:2-3, their source-language and Latin textual histories, patristic exegesis, and the bounded comparison with related biblical and Jewish material. This is a foundational historical chapter, not simply a literature review.
3. **Old English:** extraordinary bodies, radiance and the marvellous, with close attention to points of contact and distinction between the biblical repertoire and other Old English representations. Naturally limited by available corpus.
4. **Middle English:** biblical reception, exegesis, translation and encyclopaedic writing, including the *Albani* material as a bounded comparison.
5. **The early modern period, c. 1450-1650:** the reception and transformation of the sacral repertoire across religious, literary and natural-historical writing; the interaction of sacral, marvellous and naturalising framings where the evidence supports it.
6. **Comparative afterlives, c. 1650-1914:** selected natural-historical, medical and literary accounts, considered in relation to the earlier findings and to White’s study. The chapter tests transmission, transformation, residual influence, parallel development and discontinuity; it is not a comprehensive second history of albinism.
7. **Conclusion:** findings on the English reception and afterlife of the sacral white body, the limits of demonstrable connection, and the contribution and limitations of the retrieval method.

**Outputs**
The principal output is the PhD thesis. Secondary outputs (subject to copyright and licensing) will include an annotated collection of passages, a reproducible retrieval pipeline, and articles for digital humanities, literary and disability studies. Any publicly released material will document provenance, annotation decisions and limitations.

**Three-year plan**

| Period | Work |
|---|---|
| **Year 1** | Literature review; Latin training and advice on bounded source-language passages; biblical and Old English searches and close readings; define and pilot the object and annotation scheme; assemble and assess corpora (including a nineteenth-century source); compile seed lexicons; build lexical and sentence-embedding baselines and the gold set; test controls; review the scale of Middle English and comparative work at year end. |
| **Year 2** | Two-ended retrieval and evaluation of fine-tuning or hybrid methods where justified; validate the annotated sample; draft the biblical and Old English chapters; close-reading case studies in Middle English and the early modern period; trace documented reception. |
| **Year 3** | Complete the Middle English and early-modern chapters; undertake the bounded c. 1650-1914 comparison; write the conclusion and synthesis; complete the thesis; prepare outputs and release code, data and (rights permitting) corpus. |

The core historical work and evaluation against the lexical baseline take priority over optional technical extensions. The scale of the Middle English and later comparative material will be adjusted in line with the Year-1 review. The later period is deliberately comparative and can be narrowed further without compromising the principal thesis.

### 7. Risks and limitations

- **Breadth.** Mitigation: explicit core/contextual/excluded distinction (Section 3.5), Year-1 review,  and comparative material that can be reduced.
- **Method performance.** MacBERTh may not outperform a lexical baseline. Mitigation: comparative baselines and, if useful, a hybrid design; the thesis’s claims will rest on close reading, with retrieval serving as support.
- **Sparse evidence.** Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, cautious lexicon expansion and close reading where counts are low.
- **Corpus and OCR bias.** EEBO-TCP is hand-keyed; later material may contain OCR errors. MacBERTh’s training set includes EEBO and ECCO material, which helps mitigate orthographic and OCR noise, as does the nature of vector clustering; residual limitations will be documented.
- **Unstable object.** Age-related whiteness and core/associated feature distinctions may remain ambiguous. Mitigation: borderline categories and sensitivity tests.
- **False continuity.** Similar descriptions may be mistaken for transmission. Mitigation: separate resemblance from documented reception; leave unresolved relationships unresolved.
- **Circularity.** Seed descriptions shape what the system finds. Mitigation: two-ended retrieval, controls, analysis of false positives and validation through close reading.
- **Limits of the record.** Texts cannot show how representative their depictions were of ordinary encounters.
- **Language competence.** The researcher has some competency in scriptural Hebrew, Modern German and Scandinavian languages, Old and Middle English, and is comfortable with specialist editions of Germanic languages. Basic familiarity with Latin is addressed by a bounded scope and Year-1 training (Section 3.2). Specialist advice will be sought where necessary for the select passages in ancient Semitic languages, Greek and Latin.

### 8. Impact and beneficiaries

The research will be relevant to people with albinism and their families, educators, and organisations concerned with albinism and minority representation. Although rare, albinism is more common that its public underrepresentation might suggest. No reliable UK-wide epidemiological prevalence figure has been established but by way of comparison, Kromberg, Flynn and Kerr (2023) report a mean rate of 1 in 13,000 in the three European countries with adequate large-sample studies. 

By investigating a wider historical range of bodily descriptions, the project will assess whether the surviving record complicates or challenges predominantly negative contemporary accounts of this substantial but often unseen minority.

Findings will be communicated in accessible language. Opportunities will be explored to consult organisations such as Standing Voice and national albinism support groups on the presentation and dissemination of results. 

Academic beneficiaries include scholars of literary history, the history of the body and disability, biblical reception, and digital humanities methods for sparse historical phenomena. The reproducible retrieval pipeline and annotated sample (rights permitting) will constitute reusable resources.

### 9. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who has lived with albinism for over fifty years. This informs the choice of questions and an awareness of the consequences of historical representation, but does not determine the meanings found in the evidence. 

The project does not assume that albinism or extraordinary paleness has an inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that writers, communities and traditions attached to visibly marked bodies. Evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher’s own judgement will be kept distinct.
